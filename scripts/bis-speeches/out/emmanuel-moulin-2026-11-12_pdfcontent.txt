Competitiveness and resilience in
the financial sector: oxymoron or
complementarity?

Ladies and gentlemen, 
I am delighted to welcome you to this new edition of the ACPR
Conference. The theme of this gathering, “Competitiveness and
resilience in the financial sector: oxymoron or complementarity?”, is
particularly timely in today’s global economic and financial environment,
marked by uncertainty and profound geopolitical, technological and
climate-related shifts. However, I would like to look beyond the apparent
contradiction and show that resilience and competitiveness are in fact
mutually reinforcing objectives. I will begin by outlining the challenges
posed by today’s macroeconomic environment (1), before explaining how
the European financial sector’s resilience rests on three requirements (2),
and then sharing three convictions on how to strengthen its
competitiveness and resilience in the years ahead (3). 
1. The macroeconomic context represents a new test 
The current macroeconomic context represents a new test for the
European financial sector. Since the summer, sovereign bond yields have
risen in all advanced economies, driven by a combination of factors: the
ongoing conflict in the Middle East and its impact on inflation,
expectations of central bank rate increases and a sharp rise in corporate
bond issuance. In France, this upward pressure on yields has been
amplified by the uncertainty surrounding the budget. Higher sovereign
yields are increasing the cost of servicing public debt and raising
concerns about the trajectory of public finances. But we can act. It is
essential that we adopt a budget before the end of the year that brings
the government deficit down to below the threshold of 5% of GDP. This
is not just a matter for government financing: if interest rates continue to
rise, borrowing will become more expensive for both households and
businesses, which will in turn weigh on the financing of the economy and
on growth. 
Fortunately, banks and insurers are well-equipped to cope with this new
environment. They have proved their resilience in recent years by
weathering some exceptional shocks: the Covid-19 pandemic, the rapid
rise in interest rates that began in 2022, and the banking sector
turbulence of 2023. Today, they continue to fully play their role as a
shock-absorber, supporting the financing of the real economy. This
resilience is reflected in the continued flow of credit to the economy,
strong life insurance inflows and robust profitability.

2. The current resilience stems from a triple requirement 
The remarkable resilience of the banking and insurance sectors reflects
years of effort guided by three key requirements.

ACPR
The first requirement is strong rules. The international standards
adopted after the Great Financial Crisis of 2008 were implemented
faithfully and consistently across the European Union (EU). As a result,
European banks are well-positioned to navigate the current environment,
with historically high levels of capital – the average CET1 ratio of France’s
six largest banking groups stands at 15.6% – and of liquidity. Insurers are
also in a sound position, with an SCR  ratio of 250%, higher than in
previous years.

Source : ACPR

The second requirement, which is inseparable from the first, is proactive
supervision. Over the past 12 years, the Banking Union has
demonstrated the value of single European supervision and resolution.
One of its key achievements has been to improve the reliability of banks’
internal models, notably through TRIM  and the EBA’s  IRB Repair
programme. The sharp rise in interest rates in 2022 has also led to
significant improvements in banks’ management of interest rate risk.
Similar conclusions can be drawn for the insurance sector, after a
decade of implementing Solvency II.  This harmonised framework,
supported by close cooperation between national authorities and
EIOPA,  has strengthened the sector’s resilience by fostering a forward-
looking and economically grounded assessment of the risks actually
borne by insurers. The third requirement for financial institutions is the
adaptation of their business models to changing circumstances and to
long-term challenges. Today’s resilience stems from the prudence with
which banks and insurers navigated the ultra-low interest rate
environment of the mid-2010s and early 2020s. Circumstances evolve,
but the same clear-sighted prudence and collective discipline must
continue to prevail. 
The first challenge is, of course, technological. Banks and insurers are
constantly adapting their business models to technological change. They
must continue to innovate, drawing on the dynamism of the fintech
ecosystem and making full use of advances in artificial intelligence (AI).
AI offers promising use cases, be this in customer relations, with agentic
payments,  in internal process automation or in the fight against money
laundering. 
The second major challenge is climate change. The wildfires of last
summer were a stark reminder of the reality of physical risks.  They
demonstrated insurers’ ability to mobilise rapidly in support of
policyholders. But beyond this immediate response, the growing cost
and frequency of climate-related disasters underscore the scale of the
structural sustainability challenges we face. The ACPR will be attentive
to the insurance sector’s ability to provide a coordinated response,
combining stronger prevention efforts in the short term and adaptation
by policyholders and local authorities over the longer term. This effort is
essential to maintain effective insurance coverage over time.
The third challenge is that of European financial sovereignty. This implies
building a genuine Savings and Investments Union (SIU), in which banks
and insurers are essential players. Although bank lending remains the
predominant source of financing in Europe, we can still improve our

We must also increase our equity financing  to better channel European
savings towards innovative investment. The Solvency II review
contributes to this by freeing up capital for strategic investments and
easing prudential requirements for insurers’ long-term equity holdings.
Innovations linked to tokenisation can also play an important role. The
Pythagore project led by the Banque de France and Euroclear France
provides a clear illustration of this potential. By the end of 2026, central
bank money tokens will make it possible to settle tokenised NEU CPs,
paving the way for more efficient and more integrated European financial
markets. 
This triple requirement, comprising solid rules, proactive supervision and
the adaptation of business models, has enabled us to build a resilient
European financial system. In an environment marked by geopolitical,
climate-related and technological shifts, this resilience is also a source of
competitiveness. Far from being at odds, resilience and competitiveness
are in fact mutually reinforcing. It is precisely because it is resilient that
our financial system can innovate, finance the economy and strengthen
Europe’s strategic autonomy. This observation leads me to the three
convictions that I would like to share about the future of Europe’s
financial sector.

3. A triple conviction for the future of the European financial sector 
xi

The first conviction is that we must preserve trust, which is the bedrock
of both European innovation and sovereignty. Clear, predictable and
proportionate regulation is one of the foundations of that trust. It
supports innovation while safeguarding financial stability. The EU has
demonstrated this by adopting pioneering frameworks such as the MiCA
regulation for crypto-assets and the DORA regulation on digital
operational resilience. 
Central banks also have a decisive role to play in supporting and guiding
the transformation of the financial sector. This is the purpose of the work
undertaken by the Eurosystem on central bank digital currency (CBDC),
both in its retail and wholesale versions, as well as the revisions made to
the monetary policy operational framework. As part of these changes,
since March 2026, the Eurosystem has accepted certain DLT-based
marketable securities as eligible collateral for monetary policy
operations. 
But trust cannot be built by public authorities alone. It also depends on
the ability of financial sector participants to develop sovereign European
solutions, to limit our reliance on non-European critical service providers.
This can be illustrated by recent private-sector initiatives in the payment
solutions market. I welcome in particular the tie-up between EPI  and
the EuroPA consortium, which marks an important step towards a more
integrated and sovereign European payments ecosystem. 
The second conviction is that we must continue simplifying the
European regulatory framework, which has become overly complex,
but without conflating simplification with deregulation.
As I noted earlier, the EU was right to implement international standards.
It must continue to be an ambitious and demanding jurisdiction, as
illustrated by its implementation of the Basel III framework. Simplification
should not mean weakening the framework; it should make it clearer and
more consistent.

A number of simplification measures have already been implemented,
notably in supervision. Through its Next Level Supervision initiative,
 the SSM has increased efficiency, improved risk targeting thanks to
digitalisation, standardised and accelerated processes, and begun
rationalising the ECB’s external guidances. 
I also welcome the overall direction and proposals set out in the
European Commission’s July 2026 report on banking competitiveness.
The report notably recommends clarifying capital requirements (capital
stack) and removing certain EU-specific overlaps and gold-plating
measures where they are not justified by the underlying risks. We should
be pleased that it explores options to durably mitigate the impact of the
Basel output floor for low-risk activities such as residential real estate,
and proposes adjusting the treatment of certain competitive activities
that are important for financing the European economy – such as
specialised lending. In insurance, the review of the Solvency II directive,
which will enter into force in early 2027, has strengthened the existing
proportionality framework by adapting certain regulatory requirements to
the size and risk profile of institutions. 
My third conviction is that the resilience and competitiveness of Europe’s
banking sector must be shored up by completing Europe’s financial
integration. Europe has a single market, but it does not yet have a truly
single banking market. Cross-border activities remain limited: direct
intra-euro area cross-border lending accounted for less than 15% of euro
area bank lending to non-banks in 2024.   At the same time, cross-
border mergers have been relatively rare in recent years, with M&A
activity remaining largely domestic. Yet the size of European banks limits
their ability to invest massively in new technologies. Such investments
involve high fixed costs, which are easier for large institutions to absorb.
We must therefore remove the political and regulatory obstacles that
hinder the creation of cross-border European champions. Despite the
undeniable success of the SSM and the Single Resolution Mechanism
(SRM), European banking groups are still too often required to manage
their capital and liquidity according to national criteria. We should
therefore do more to encourage the consolidated management of equity
and liquidity within cross-border banking groups. The time has come to
introduce cross-border capital and liquidity waivers, without making
them conditional on finalising the European Deposit Insurance Scheme
(EDIS).

The current environment is putting the French and European economies
to the test. But our financial sector is entering this period in a position of
strength. The strong rules introduced since the Great Financial Crisis,
proactive supervision and the adaptation of business models have
enabled us to build a resilient banking and insurance sector. This
resilience must continue to support our competitiveness, our ability to
innovate and our sovereignty. This is why we must maintain a clear
course: preserving trust, simplifying without deregulating and completing
European financial integration. Only by meeting these three conditions
can banks and insurers fully finance the real economy, support the
transformations underway and strengthen Europe’s strategic autonomy.
