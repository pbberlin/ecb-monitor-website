Executive summary 
The public consultation on the Appia Roadmap, conducted between 11 March 
and 22 April 2026, confirms broad market support for the development of an 
integrated, innovative and resilient European tokenised finance ecosystem 
anchored in central bank money. The consultation sought to gather feedback from 
a diverse range of private and public stakeholders, to ensure that the strategic 
direction aligns with both market demand and public policy objectives and to identify 
opportunities for public-private collaboration. This report summarises the key findings 
from the market feedback, which will form the basis for strong and continuous 
engagement in the years to come. It does not reflect any Eurosystem preference on 
the design or architecture options for Appia, which remain open and will be assessed 
as part of the work leading to the Appia blueprint in 2028. The Eurosystem will in the 
meantime communicate on the progress made in Appia. 
The consultation received 127 responses from a diverse group of European 
and international stakeholders. The respondents included financial institutions, 
market infrastructures, technology providers, industry associations, public 
authorities, academics, advisers and individual experts. The feedback was largely 
supportive of the Roadmap, with many respondents making proposals for additional 
practical work or contributions. 
There was broad support for the high-level principles set out in the Appia 
Roadmap, both those relating to market functioning and those of a more 
technical and operational nature. Specifically, the role of central bank money as 
an anchor for the tokenised ecosystem was widely endorsed, including its role in 
connecting and ensuring convertibility between the different private settlement 
assets that play a complementary role. Respondents also recognised the importance 
of central bank involvement in collateral management and liquidity routing. Most 
respondents supported a public-private collaboration model, acknowledging a 
significant role for the Eurosystem 0F
1 while also leaving room for private sector 
development and innovation. Safeguards were proposed to limit market power 
concentration and prevent vendor lock-in. Respondents also emphasised the 
challenges of the transition towards tokenised finance, stressing that any 
expectations placed on new infrastructure providers should be aligned with those 
placed on existing market infrastructures. 
Most respondents supported the strategic autonomy objectives set out in the 
Appia Roadmap and called for harmonised legal and regulatory frameworks 
across the European Union. Respondents stressed that autonomy must also be

The development of the network layer as a shared utility layer was viewed 
positively by most respondents, albeit with diverse views on what the layer 
should cover. Most respondents considered that ledger management, transaction 
settlement, identity and access management and security should be included within 
this layer as basic infrastructure features. In keeping with the approach set out in the 
Appia roadmap, aspects related to commercial activities – e.g. client-facing services, 
risk transformation, business model or value-added services – were viewed as better 
managed outside of the network layer. Specific views notwithstanding, it was 
emphasised that the network layer should not necessarily be limited to a single layer 
of infrastructure, but rather could comprise a network of layers operated across a set 
of actors. A strong governance model based on clear and objective criteria would be 
needed to ensure neutrality.  
In terms of architecture, rather than converging on a single preferred model, 
respondents identified a trade-off. Enabling central bank money settlement on one 
network was seen by many respondents as the most effective way to reduce 
fragmentation, concentrate liquidity, simplify governance and standardise processes, 
albeit at the risk of increasing concentration and technology lock-in risks and 
weakening incentives to innovate. Conversely, a multi-network ecosystem – which 
many other respondents considered the more likely market outcome, and the one 
best able to preserve central bank money's role as the settlement anchor across 
emerging networks – was seen as more resilient and more accommodating of 
diverse business models, albeit at the cost of greater interoperability requirements 
and a higher risk of fragmenting liquidity, collateral and compliance. Many 
respondents also proposed an alternative approach: enabling central bank money 
settlement on a limited number of eligible networks, subject to transparent eligibility 
criteria and common standards. This was described as a realistic medium-term 
configuration by several respondents and as a credible long-term target by others. 
The feasibility of central bank money settlement on more than one network, in 
particular the Eurosystem's ability to retain control over central bank money, is still to 
be assessed as part of Appia. 
Standardisation as a means of supporting the development of a tokenised 
finance ecosystem was a key topic for the majority of respondents. It was 
argued that development should start with the foundational layers, before gradually 
building up to more complex domains. Where possible, the Eurosystem should focus 
on codifying existing practices within the sector. In other areas, experimentation and 
research should be promoted to develop new standards. Delays in developing 
standards could have a significant impact, some respondents suggested, as the 
sector could evolve into a fragmented landscape. Many respondents also stressed 
that standards should explicitly address interoperability between DLT-based and

in parallel over a lengthy transition period. 
There was a high degree of willingness among respondents to contribute 
actively to Appia. Suggestions included contributions to analytical work, as well as

working on Appia welcomed this robust engagement. The feedback and proposals 
for contribution will be used to involve the market in the upcoming design and the

public-private partnership. As an initial step, market participants will be 
encouraged to continue developing and testing tokenised assets and services

progress of Appia. The strong response to the consultation – in particular on asset

a substantial pool of expertise to help shape it. The next stage will translate this

1 Introduction 
The ECB published the Appia Roadmap 2F
3 on 11 March 2026, setting out the 
Eurosystem’s approach to Appia, its strategic initiative to shape a European 
tokenised finance ecosystem to foster integration, innovation and growth. The 
Roadmap describes the objectives of the initiative, the principles that guide it and the 
building blocks through which it will be delivered. 
A first step towards creating this ecosystem has already been taken with the 
launch of Pontes, which enables the safe settlement of DLT-based wholesale 
transactions in central bank money. The broader promise of DLT and tokenised 
finance extends to potentially redesigning the entire transaction lifecycle, enhancing 
efficiency and innovation, well beyond settlement. To this end, the Eurosystem will 
deliver a blueprint for a future European tokenised ecosystem by 2028, covering the 
entire value chain and a wider range of use cases beyond settlement. 
The success of such a transformative initiative depends on collaboration 
between the public and private sectors. The Eurosystem appreciates the efforts 
required for such a large-scale transformation and places great value on an open 
dialogue with the market.  
The Appia Roadmap was therefore open for public consultation between 11 
March and 22 April 2026. The consultation consisted of 18 open-ended questions 
organised into two sections: (1) feedback on the Appia Roadmap; and (2) proposals 
for contributions to Appia. 
Part 1 addressed the following topics: 
• high-level principles; 
• network layer and tokenised market infrastructures as a neutral shared utility; 
• strategic autonomy; 
• common standards, rules and practices; 
• central bank money settlement on a single or multiple networks; 
• core service provision by the Eurosystem on a directly owned network, or 
shared governance;  
• market and public sector engagement. 
The full responses to Part 1 are published on the ECB website.  
Part 2 covered possible contributions towards Appia Building Blocks (ABBs) 1-6: 
• ABB 1 – Asset interoperability and standards;

• ABB 2 – Monetary policy implementation and collateral management on DLT; 
• ABB 3 – European tokenised central bank money infrastructure(s); 
• ABB 4 – The international dimension and cross-border links; 
• ABB 5 – An innovative, safe and resilient ecosystem;  
• ABB 6 – Implementation strategy and impact on existing infrastructures. 
As announced in the public consultation, the responses to Part 2 are confidential and

This report focuses on the lessons drawn from Part 1 of the consultation and 
incorporates feedback from 127 respondents from across the financial sector, 
covering all or part of the questions posed. Respondents included credit 
institutions, investment firms, investors and buy-side firms, issuers of financial 
assets, trading platforms, post-trade infrastructures, private settlement asset and 
payment infrastructure providers, technical and data providers, professional 
associations and standards initiatives, public authorities, academics, specialised 
consultants and experts replying in a personal capacity.  
This report reflects market feedback only. It does not reflect the Eurosystem's 
views, nor does it express any preference on the design and architecture 
options for Appia, which remain open. The feedback will serve as an input into 
the Appia blueprint to be delivered by 2028, without prejudging its outcome. The 
Eurosystem will in the meantime communicate on the progress made in Appia. 
The remainder of the report is structured as follows. Section 2 provides 
descriptive statistics on the results of the consultation. Section 3 sets out the 
feedback on the high-level principles and desired policy outcomes. Section 4 turns to 
architecture options and designs. Section 5 presents the feedback on common 
standards, rules and practices. Section 6 looks at the way forward, including how the 
feedback will be incorporated into the design of Appia, market interest in contributing 
to the Appia Building Blocks and the next steps for engaging with the market. 
Sections 3, 4 and 5 each open with a recap of the relevant key points of the Appia 
Roadmap before summarising the views expressed by respondents.

3 Feedback on high-level principles and desired policy outcomes 
Respondents were first asked for feedback on the proposed high-level principles 
guiding future development, on the potential of the network layer as a common 
shared utility and on their views on European strategic autonomy and how Appia can 
further this goal.   
3.1 Recapping the key points from the Roadmap 
The Appia Roadmap first sets out four objectives: 
• ensuring the effectiveness of monetary policy and financial stability and the 
smooth functioning of payment systems by maintaining central bank money as 
the anchor of a two-tier monetary system; 
• fostering a more integrated, competitive and innovative payments and securities 
ecosystem through efficient infrastructures for financial markets; 
• supporting strategic autonomy and increased resilience;  
• ensuring the relevance of the euro as an international currency.

The Roadmap then sets out a series of high-level principles to guide progress 
towards a future-ready solution. It also provides a conceptual basis for Appia, 
distinguishing between a network layer (the basic infrastructure) and an assets and 
services layer, each of which has its own governance arrangements (e.g. who 
operates the layer, who can access it and who can see the data). The network layer 
would: 
• function as a shared utility, providing all participants with equal and non-
discriminatory access, thereby enabling innovation and competition at the 
assets and services layer; 
• support strategic autonomy by relying primarily on utilities that are located, 
operated and governed in Europe and are subject to European legal and 
regulatory frameworks.  
3.2 Broad support for Appia’s high-level principles 
Respondents placed the greatest emphasis on the role of central bank money 
as the monetary anchor of the system. This emphasis was expressed at two 
levels: as a policy objective – central bank money should remain the ultimate 
settlement asset underpinning the tokenised ecosystem – and as a design 
requirement – the architecture of Appia should be built so that central bank money 
settlement is available to participants by default, rather than as an add-on.  
Respondents also saw a key role for the central bank in several functions 
beyond settlement. The Eurosystem will further develop these in light of the 
results of Appia, building on Pontes. In collateral management, respondents 
pointed to the central bank's role in accepting and mobilising tokenised collateral for 
credit operations. On liquidity, they highlighted the need to ensure that central bank 
money balances can move efficiently between DLT platforms and existing TARGET 
Services, to mitigate the risk of liquidity becoming fragmented across venues.  
The anchor role of central bank money was valued because it strengthens 
monetary sovereignty, ensures settlement finality, provides legal certainty and 
improves systemic resilience. Respondents also highlighted the way the central 
bank provides its services under a public mandate, in a market-neutral fashion, 
ensuring connectivity of market stakeholders across the wholesale ecosystem, 
through harmonised access to central bank money as the ultimate safe settlement 
asset. 
A two-tier system combining tokenised central bank money with private 
settlement assets was viewed favourably by many respondents, as a design 
considered both more feasible and more conducive to innovation. In reference 
to tokenised deposits and (euro-denominated) stablecoins as complementary assets, 
respondents noted that these tokenised assets could further support the 
development of additional or more advanced use cases. In any event, the singleness 
of money should be ensured and the hierarchy of assets should remain clear.

Respondents expressed strong support for the principles of market access 
and integration and competition and contestability, but stressed that these can 
only be achieved if barriers to entry are actively addressed. Competition 
concerns rivalry among existing providers, while contestability refers to the ability of 
new providers to enter the market and challenge incumbents on equal terms. 
Several respondents noted that low interoperability between networks, the absence 
of common industry standards and the emergence of walled gardens would, in 
practice, entrench incumbents and lock participants into single platforms. A number 
of respondents therefore asked that contestability be treated as a concrete design 
requirement rather than a general aspiration. This feedback confirms Appia’s focus 
on the implications of DLT adoption for market functioning, economic efficiency and 
governance as equally important to its technical considerations. 
Robustness, legal certainty, innovation and governance and control were also 
seen as key principles, which many respondents argued should be 
functionally equivalent to those that apply to existing financial market 
infrastructures. Respondents identified several areas that require specific treatment 
given the nature of DLT, such as the legal finality of on-chain settlement, the 
treatment of smart contract execution and the allocation of liability where such 
execution fails, key management and custody of cryptographic credentials and the 
governance of protocol upgrades and forks. Some respondents stressed that the 
interaction with existing financial market infrastructures should also be taken into 
consideration, as the transition towards tokenised finance is expected to happen 
gradually, and that the implementation of Appia should therefore ensure a degree of 
backward compatibility and pragmatism with regard to TARGET Services, central 
securities depositories and other existing financial market infrastructures.  
A limited number of additional principles were proposed, with interoperability 
the most frequently cited by far. Several respondents nevertheless considered 
that interoperability was already covered by the existing principles.  
The other most frequently proposed additions concerned privacy and 
confidentiality, on the one hand, and transparency, compliance and 
auditability, on the other – two sets of requirements that pull in opposite 
directions. To ensure the privacy and confidentiality of positions, counterparties and 
transaction terms, data must be shielded from other participants on a shared ledger. 
Meanwhile, supervisory reporting, auditability and AML/CFT compliance mean that 
transactions must be verifiable by third parties. While this tension is not specific to 
DLT, respondents noted that it becomes more acute when records are validated and 
replicated across multiple participants, rather than held by a single trusted operator. 
Several respondents therefore suggested that Appia should not choose between the 
two, but rather should set out explicitly how they are to be reconciled – e.g. through 
selective disclosure, permissioned data visibility or privacy-enhancing technologies 
such as zero-knowledge proofs – and that the allocation of visibility rights should be 
treated as a matter of governance, not simply a technical issue.  
Several respondents also suggested adding cross-border connectivity and 
proportionality of participation requirements, i.e. ensuring that market participants of 
different sizes and with different scopes of activity are able to participate.

Many respondents translated these principles into concrete requirements for the 
architecture of the ecosystem. These are presented together with the architecture 
feedback in Section 4. 
3.3 The network layer as a shared utility 
There was overall support for the concept of the network layer as a shared 
utility, although views differed on its nature. While many respondents supported 
the idea of a neutral, shared layer, they considered that the precise elements to be 
included need further elaboration. Most respondents favoured a (limited) 
coordination layer allowing users to connect to various services, rather than a 
monolithic ledger or vertically integrated platform. In their view, the network layer 
does not necessarily imply a single layer or infrastructure, but could instead consist 
of a landscape of multiple connected networks or layers established under a 
common framework.  
Within the network layer, respondents identified a number of elements that 
could realistically function as a shared utility: 
• the basic infrastructure providing core functions, such as ledger integrity, 
consensus and validation; 
• functions relating to transaction settlement, in particular but not only in central 
bank money, and including multi-leg transactions, such as delivery-versus-
payment and payment-versus payment; 
• functions relating to identity and access management; 
• functions relating to regulatory requirements and compliance, such as reporting, 
attestations and auditability; 
• standards, including for tokens, smart contracts and messaging; 
• interoperability services, frameworks, interfaces and gateways;  
• functions relating to (cyber-)security and resilience. 
To ensure neutrality, a number of respondents pointed out the need for 
operational criteria as well as enforcement. Open and non-discriminatory access 
based on objective criteria would be required, alongside a technology-agnostic 
solution for the broadest possible participation across the market. 
Governance would be another core element, ensuring in particular that the 
operator or governing body of this layer does not compete commercially with 
other entities. Some respondents mentioned a cooperative model, whereby 
participants using the network would hold governance rights, as a way of delivering 
accountability and neutrality. Irrespective of the model chosen, respondents called 
for a clear delineation between the network layer and the competitive services 
offered within the ecosystem.

In line with the concept set out in the Appia Roadmap, respondents generally 
considered commercially driven functions unsuitable for operation as a shared 
utility. These include value-added services; commercial and product decisions such 
as pricing and product design; balance-sheet intermediation and risk transformation; 
and client-facing services such as issuance, trading, custody, asset servicing, 
corporate actions, the provision of wallets and client onboarding. These are areas in 
which providers should be able to differentiate themselves and compete. Placing 
them in a shared utility would put the utility in competition with its own users, 
undermining the neutrality that justifies its existence in the first place. 
There was therefore broad consensus that the assets and services layer 
should be contestable, with competition focused on the quality and pricing of 
services, as also stated in the Appia Roadmap. Views differed as to the extent to 
which the network layer should be contestable, and as to how far a common base 
infrastructure is needed in order to share key functions in an integrated ecosystem. 
3.4 Strategic autonomy in theory and in practice 
Respondents supported the objective of strategic autonomy overall, but held 
different views on how best to achieve it. A major theme among the responses 
was resilience, meaning the ability to continue providing services irrespective of 
failures or other barriers resulting from actors outside the EU.  
A clear baseline emerged with regard to the legal dimension: core 
infrastructures and critical functions should operate under harmonised EU 
legal and regulatory frameworks and should, where relevant, be subject to EU 
supervision or oversight. Respondents considered that the interaction between 
Appia and existing regulations (e.g. the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation,3F
4 the 
Central Securities Depository Regulation 4F
5 and the Settlement Finality Directive 5F6) 
should be clarified. In some areas, further legislative acts would be required to 
ensure full harmonisation or cover specific gaps resulting from tokenisation. For 
example, respondents suggested that proof of compliance should be embedded in 
the tokenised assets themselves, rather than relying on self-certification. 
European governance of core infrastructures and critical functions – or at 
least majority European shareholding and majority European representation in 
decision-making bodies – was also seen as a key element of strategic 
autonomy. Several respondents considered this essential to the ability to set 
European rules on access and operations and to ensure that resilience requirements 
are met. Some respondents also argued that critical infrastructures and/or their 
operators should be located in Europe. In the same vein, they pointed to

Central bank money settlement came in for particular attention, both as an 
important element of strategic autonomy in itself and because respondents 
considered that effective control over the issuance, holding and transfer of 
tokenised central bank money must remain with the Eurosystem. This must be 
secured by design, through legal, contractual, technical and operational means. 
Different ways of ensuring this control were put forward, which depend on 
architecture choices, as explained in Section 4. Some respondents added that, 
alongside tokenised central bank money, euro-denominated private settlement 
assets such as stablecoins and tokenised deposits should also be supported, so that 
a broad range of euro-based settlement options is available. 
One key theme in the replies was the need to avoid dependence – not only on 
non-European providers, but more generally on any single provider for critical 
functions, or on a concentration of control among very few entities. Open 
standards and open-source protocols, as well as portability and interoperability 
between providers, were put forward as means of preventing such dependencies. 
Respondents noted in particular that open-source code reduces vendor dependency 
precisely because it is auditable, portable and can be forked, but that its contribution 
to strategic autonomy can only be fully realised if European stakeholders are also 
present in the governance and maintenance of the relevant projects. Another 
prevalent theme was the importance of ensuring that the relevant technological 
capabilities are available in Europe, e.g. through R&D funding and skills 
development. 
Finally, many respondents stressed that the pursuit of European sovereignty 
should not lead to isolation. Interoperability with the rest of the world was seen as 
essential, including for the attractiveness of the European market and the euro. 
Respondents warned that the efficiency of monetary policy transmission could be 
affected if the market came to rely on foreign-denominated currencies or liquidity 
pools. 
4 Feedback on architecture options and designs 
Across the responses, there was clear common ground that central bank money 
should serve as the settlement anchor for the tokenised ecosystem. However,

respondents underlined key trade-offs in translating this anchor role into a concrete 
architecture for Appia, given the fundamental uncertainties over technological and 
market developments. 
4.1 Recapping the key points from the Roadmap 
The Appia Roadmap deliberately keeps architecture open, presenting it as a 
set of options to be assessed holistically against Appia’s objectives. 
One of the roadmap's foundational objectives is to maintain central bank 
money as the anchor of the system, thereby safeguarding the effectiveness of 
monetary policy, financial stability and the smooth functioning of payment 
systems. A key question expressly left open is whether the requirement for central 
bank control over central bank money can be met under different infrastructure 
configurations and, if so, how. 
Three key interrelated areas bear directly on architecture: 
• Interoperability and standards (ABB 1). Whether assets can be transferred 
across market DLT platforms, requiring both suitable interoperability and asset 
transfer mechanisms and compatible standards and data formats across 
platforms. 
• Network configuration (ABB 2). The assessment of network configuration 
variants for monetary policy implementation and collateral management, 
including whether – and how – to control mobilised assets on DLT not fully 
operated by the Eurosystem, and whether a single platform for collateral 
mobilisation is required. 
• Infrastructure type, number and scope (ABB 3). The type(s), number and 
scope of infrastructures on which tokenised central bank money is provided – 
covering as a minimum the euro and other TARGET currencies – including 
whether they should host central bank money only or also investment assets 
such as securities and/or regulated private settlement assets. Governance, the 
role of the Eurosystem and the impact on existing TARGET Services and their 
development relative to market players are additional core considerations. 
The Roadmap also emphasises the international dimension and cross-border 
links (ABB 4) and the need for a safe and resilient ecosystem (ABB 5), 
including the risks that fragmentation and divergence in technological and 
operational standards could pose to financial stability. All of this must be taken 
into account when designing the architecture of the Appia ecosystem.

4.2 The number of networks on which tokenised central money is 
made available 
Enabling central bank money settlement on a single network was widely 
recognised by respondents as a powerful way to reduce fragmentation, fully 
leverage the benefits of a common ledger for different assets and services, 
simplify governance, concentrate liquidity and standardise processes. 
Respondents noted that this could have a positive impact on efficiency, reduce 
duplicated investment and lower coordination costs. Several respondents outlined 
the robust governance, resilience, access and security frameworks required to make 
a single network viable in the medium and long term. 
At the same time, respondents highlighted the drawbacks of a common ledger: 
greater concentration of activity, the systemic importance that such a network 
would inevitably acquire, the risk of long-term lock-in into a specific 
technology or vendor and potentially weaker incentives to innovate. 
Respondents pointed out that such a design could discourage market 
experimentation. Many respondents nevertheless took a positive view of a single 
network. In particular, it was seen as the best way to achieve some of the objectives 
in the Appia Roadmap: mainly, reducing market and liquidity fragmentation and 
maintaining neutrality. 
Several respondents noted that the ecosystem is currently developing on 
multiple networks and warned against limiting the usability of central bank 
money to a single or narrow set of networks that cannot fully meet market 
needs. In their view, this would create a risk that other settlement assets not 
anchored on central bank money would service those needs on other networks, 
thereby weakening the role of central bank money as the settlement anchor and 
increasing fragmentation across settlement assets. 
Many respondents therefore described enabling central bank money 
settlement on a limited number of selected networks as a realistic medium-
term configuration and, for several, a credible long-term target. This would 
reflect today’s multi-network reality, foster competition and innovation and reduce 
single-point concentration risk, while still allowing the Eurosystem to set clear and 
transparent eligibility requirements and promote common standards and oversight 
expectations across the eligible networks. It would require strict, transparent 
eligibility criteria, contestability and interoperability and standardisation across all 
these networks. Such an approach would balance the resilience and adaptability of 
distinct networks operating in parallel with the need to ensure interoperability and the 
mobility of assets and liquidity across networks. Respondents identified 
fragmentation as the main challenge of this approach. In their assessment, without 
robust and enforceable interoperability standards and common semantics, a multi-
network design risks becoming a set of silos that fragment liquidity, collateral, identity 
and compliance.

4.3 The choice between a proprietary or a shared network  
Respondents did not converge on a single preference between a network 
operated by the Eurosystem itself and the provision of Eurosystem services 
on networks operated by the market. They saw merit in both approaches, 
provided the Eurosystem retains full control over central bank money. 
Respondents acknowledged the value of the Eurosystem providing its own network 
from where it operates its core services, as this could provide legal certainty, 
settlement finality and a neutral basis for connectivity to the wider ecosystem and 
existing infrastructures. They also underlined that the benefits of such a network 
would be greater if it were designed as an interoperable foundation for the wider 
ecosystem, enabling market stakeholders to develop value-added services and, 
where appropriate, programmable functionalities. 
Equally, respondents stressed the benefits of the Eurosystem providing its 
services on multiple eligible networks to best meet market needs, while 
acknowledging that the central bank would need to specify governance requirements 
to retain control over central bank money and fulfil its mandate. The bottom line 
drawn by respondents is that network ownership is less decisive than the ability to 
guarantee central bank control, neutrality and open access. Such conditions were 
considered achievable, albeit with different trade-offs, under both options. 
4.4 Shared governance 
Shared governance between public and private stakeholders was seen by 
many respondents as important for unlocking the full potential of tokenisation 
in terms of market efficiency and competition. Respondents underlined the 
benefits of multiple assets, services and providers operating on common trusted 
networks that are neutral and rely on common interoperability mechanisms and open 
standards to foster competition and market efficiency in the tokenised ecosystem. 
Governance arrangements that balance the needs of public and private 
stakeholders, while maintaining legal certainty and regulatory compliance and 
ensuring clear responsibilities and separation of roles, were seen as an important 
enabler of these benefits. 
4.5 A matter of choice(s) 
Many respondents set out the pros and cons of different approaches without 
expressing a firm preference, instead emphasising the need for Appia to 
identify the preferred approach following deliberation and experimentation 
with the market. 
Irrespective of the network architecture chosen, respondents consistently translated 
the high-level principles detailed in Section 3 into a set of design requirements for 
the provision of tokenised central bank money: 

 
Eurosystem report on the public consultation on the Appia Roadmap 16 
 
 
• Neutrality and separation of roles: strict governance separation between 
network operations and the governance of central bank money, other assets 
and services. 
• Openness: open technological implementation of tokenised central bank 
money, based on open standards to ensure seamlessly interoperability with 
other networks and existing infrastructures. 
• Resilience: 24/7/365 availability, clear service level agreements, deterministic 
finality, strong observability and a credible cyber and quantum-resilience 
roadmap aligned with relevant EU operational resilience requirements. 
• Programmability: support for conditional settlement, atomic DvP and PvP, 
netting of flows and lifecycle controls, without over-extending into application-
layer services. 
• Privacy and sovereignty by design: strong identity and privacy features, read-
only regulatory access separated from operations and a European-controlled 
technology stack with governance that preserves European sovereignty. 
• Competitiveness: competition preserved where the market wants it. Many 
respondents that favour a multi-network model want competition at both the 
infrastructure and service layers. A single network model would confine 
competition to the service layer, which some respondents viewed as a 
drawback rather than a design goal. 
5 Common standards, rules and practices 
The use of common standards, rules and practices was considered one of the main 
drivers of widespread adoption and interoperability. Respondents shared their views 
on the importance of these topics, as well as on the Eurosystem’s specific role in 
their development. 
5.1 Recapping the key points from the Roadmap 
The Appia Roadmap identified the establishment of common standards, rules 
and practices as essential for creating a competitive and integrated 
ecosystem. It envisions the network layer functioning as a shared utility, while 
compatibility across providers at the assets and services layer is necessary to foster 
competition and innovation. Common standards are designed to ensure this 
compatibility. 
ABB 1 – asset interoperability and standards – reflects this approach. The 
Eurosystem will call for interoperability between market DLT platforms and 
collaborate with stakeholders to develop compatible standards and data formats for 
tokenised assets and smart contracts, with the aim of enabling smooth asset 
transfers across platforms. 

 
Eurosystem report on the public consultation on the Appia Roadmap 17 
 
 
The Appia Roadmap also emphasises the role of market participants and 
standards bodies in contributing to the standards, rules and practices required 
for an integrated ecosystem. Appia therefore builds on existing standardisation 
work rather than seeking to replace it. 
In the public consultation, feedback was requested on the best ways to establish 
common standards, rules and practices when shaping the tokenised financial 
ecosystem, the domains in which standardisation should be prioritised and the role 
public institutions should play in this regard. 
5.2 Building European standards 
The importance of common standards was acknowledged by most 
respondents across market segments as a means of preventing platform 
fragmentation and ensuring interoperability between DLT and existing market 
infrastructures. Associations and standards initiatives highlighted that a lack of 
common standards would weaken network effects and delay adoption. 
Respondents identified key domains for standardisation: 
• asset representation on DLT; 
• communication between systems and networks; 
• identification, authentication and authorisation of participants;  
• alignment of governance, compliance, access and operational rules across 
infrastructures. 
Many respondents viewed standard-setting as a multi-stakeholder process in 
which the Eurosystem’s role should vary according to the maturity of the 
market.  Where market practices have already largely started to converge, 
respondents considered that the resulting standards should be enshrined in the 
Appia rulebook. Otherwise, experimentation and research should be encouraged 
until a market standard has emerged, which can then be formally adopted within the 
Appia ecosystem. Respondents considered this approach preferable to prescriptive 
action by public authorities. In their view, public institutions should set the strategic 
direction and guardrails while leaving technical design to the market. 
Respondents expressed a preference for a staged approach, starting with the 
foundational layers that require the least effort. These would be tested through 
practical implementation, with the application of standards gradually extended to 
more complex domains as volumes and experience increase. This sequencing was 
considered optimal for balancing speed and resilience while avoiding the premature 
lock-in of immature designs. Many respondents also emphasised that emerging 
standards should support compatibility between DLT-based and existing 
infrastructures.

Identity, access and compliance were key topics due to existing issues with 
conflicting or incomplete frameworks. The issues raised concern the identification 
of both individuals and enterprises in the context of KYC/AML, sanctions screening 
and national requirements. Respondents considered that interoperable identifiers 
would strengthen interoperability between platforms or across borders, with some 
respondents arguing that this should be included in cryptographically certified or 
machine-readable format. Several respondents saw the certification of tokenised 
assets at the network level, rather than through self-certification, as a potentially 
major benefit of Appia.  
Respondents stressed that clarity of governance is as critical as technical 
design. Transparent participation criteria in standard-setting, clearly defined roles 
and responsibilities and predictable decision-making processes were seen as vital to 
maintaining trust in the standard-setting process.  
Irrespective of the specific choices made, some respondents invited the 
Eurosystem to take the role of global financial markets into account, so that 
European standards remain relevant in an international setting. In their view, 
European standards should be developed with a view to convergence, or at least 
compatibility, with emerging international practice. 
6 The way forward 
The public consultation also invited respondents to share their views on future 
collaboration and on how the Eurosystem should engage with a diverse pool of 
stakeholders. 
6.1 Incorporating market feedback into the Appia design 
The feedback received will feed into the further development of the Appia 
principles, the policy work and the architecture design. This report reflects the 
views of respondents and does not constitute a decision or preference of the 
Eurosystem on the design and architecture options for Appia, which remain open. 
The responses will be analysed in greater depth and will inform the analytical and 
practical work under the ABBs, leading to the Appia blueprint to be delivered by 
2028. Engagement with the market will continue in the context of the ABBs, as well 
as through Pontes onboarding and development. 
6.2 High market interest in contributing 
Responses show a high degree of willingness across the full range of 
stakeholders to contribute further to the design of Appia, including through 
specialised contributions to specific building blocks. The contributions proposed 
by the broad range of respondents were highly diverse, ranging from analytical 

 
Eurosystem report on the public consultation on the Appia Roadmap 19 
 
 
design and reviews to active participation in prototype development and pilot 
projects, legal and regulatory feedback and governance design.  
Respondents’ proposals were flexible and tailored to their specific activity and 
expertise, highlighting their role in the value chain and how (global) 
interoperability could be ensured across interconnected platforms spanning 
both traditional and tokenised finance. While some respondents focused more on 
design-based contributions, others offered substantial participation in implementation 
work. In particular, several respondents stressed that engagement should not be 
limited to conceptual work, but should also include real-world implementation. By 
way of example, several respondents proposed a dedicated collaborative sandbox in 
which solutions could be tested on a more hands-on basis. 
A number of stakeholders offered to work directly with the Eurosystem as part 
of an ongoing collaboration on specialised topics with leading experts. 
However, several respondents cautioned that over-reliance on individual contributors 
could distort the process. Given that stakeholders have diverse and at times 
divergent interests, diversity and representativeness were considered important as a 
safeguard against vested interests.  
ABB 1, covering asset interoperability and standards, attracted the highest 
degree of interest, with more than 70% of respondents submitting ideas for 
collaboration. The other five ABBs garnered comparatively similar levels of interest, 
with around half of the respondents expressing an interest in contributing to each 
one. 
Areas of particular focus included: 
• tokenised securities 
• tokenised private settlement assets  
• sophisticated lifecycle management of tokenised assets; 
• interoperability and transferability across networks; 
• clearing, netting and on-chain liquidity management;  
• collateral management; 
• identity, credentials and business data management; 
• governance and operation of shared networks; 
• cross-border use cases; 
• programmable compliance and sanctions screening; 
• privacy and cryptographic techniques;  
• resilience and stress testing.

These areas differ in nature, and the format for collaboration will therefore be 
adapted accordingly. Topics that are closely tied to specific products or services, 
such as the tokenisation of particular financial instruments, are best advanced by the 
actors directly concerned, with the Eurosystem drawing on the results of their efforts. 
Topics that shape the ecosystem as a whole – notably, governance arrangements, 
common standards and the shared utilities underpinning Appia – require joint work 
across stakeholder groups, since no single actor can address them alone. This 
distinction will guide how the Appia work is organised, and which stakeholders are 
involved in each workstream.  
The ECB, together with several national central banks, has begun work on the 
core central banking foundations – in particular, tokenised central bank money 
and on-chain central bank collateral mobilisation – laying the groundwork for future 
experimentation with the market. In parallel, market stakeholders are invited to 
continue developing and experimenting with their key DLT-based services and 
products, in particular for aspects where tokenised securities and tokenised private 
settlement assets still require further investigation. Sharing the lessons learned from 
these activities could support the Eurosystem in its design work. Once the individual 
components and design elements have reached sufficient maturity, more complex, 
cross-cutting testing will follow, combining and connecting the different elements as 
part of the public-private partnership envisaged under Appia and building on the 
progress achieved during this foundational phase. 
6.3 Engaging the market 
The Eurosystem intends to strengthen its engagement with the market, 
including via the new Appia Contact Group as a forum for regular dialogue 
with market participants. This group (whose members were announced on the 
ECB website in August 2026) began its work in September 2026. The aim of this 
group is to provide the Eurosystem with informed feedback and to act as a sounding 
board on aspects linked to the Eurosystem’s initiatives on tokenisation, namely 
Pontes and Appia. Meeting documentation of the Appia Contact Group is made 
publicly available on the ECB website. 
In parallel, a stakeholder group supporting the creation of European market-
wide standards is being set up. A call for nomination is expected to be launched in 
due course, with further details to be communicated. 
The Eurosystem also intends to involve market participants in analytical and 
practical work, starting before the end of 2026, with a view to informing the 
Appia blueprint. Based on the feedback and proposals received in the public 
consultation, the Eurosystem has identified several areas of interest for such work. 
As a next step, the Eurosystem will launch a call for stakeholders to work on three 
priority topics: tokenised deposits and payments, tokenised securities and shared 
network governance. The aim is to progress the design of these topics jointly, 
building on ongoing market developments, including through joint experiments where

applicable. The modalities of this work, including its scope and the selection of 
participants, will be communicated in due course. 
Engagement will extend beyond the Appia Contact Group and the 
standardisation group. Other engagement formats, such as deep dives, workshops 
and hands-on experimentation, may also be used. Stakeholders are encouraged to 
keep the ECB and their national central bank informed of their progress, so that they 
can be involved in the relevant fora. Regular public communication on the progress 
of the Appia initiative, such as Focus Sessions, will be provided via the ECB website.
