Abstract
Artificial intelligence is transforming financial markets by enabling investors
and intermediaries to extract more information from increasingly abundant
data through advances in algorithms and computing power. We discuss how
AI changes information production and decision-making, labor demand, and
financial intermediation, with a particular emphasis on securities markets.
We then survey the implications of this transformation for market efficiency,
competition, informational frictions, and financial stability.
Keywords: artificial intelligence, market efficiency, agency conflicts, competi-
tion, financial stability.
JEL Codes: G1, G14, G23, G28, E6.

Non-technical summary
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly changing the finance industry. Financial intermedi-
aries are investing heavily in data, computing infrastructure, algorithms, and specialized
staff. Survey evidence shows that AI adoption is widespread and accelerating, especially
amonglargefirmsandthoseactiveinsecuritiesmarkets. Moreover, financejobsareamong
those most exposed to AI-driven automation. This is not surprising because information is
at the heart of almost every financial activity. Financial intermediaries collect and process
information to assess investments, manage risk, and allocate capital. By combining large
and diverse datasets with increasingly powerful algorithms and computing capabilities,
AI enables them to make more accurate predictions and automate decision-making.
This development raises important questions for economists and policymakers. Will
AI make markets more efficient and help direct capital to its most productive uses? Or
will it concentrate power in the hands of a small circle of tech-savvy firms? Will it
make financial information more accessible to everyone, or instead increase the advantage
of sophisticated institutions that can afford proprietary data, advanced algorithms, and
large computing resources? And how might these changes affect financial stability?
Thepaperiscenteredaroundtheideathatfinancialintermediariesusedataandhuman
expertise to produce information and make decisions, such as evaluating investments and
constructing portfolios. AI reshapes this process by increasing the productivity of data:
better algorithms and greater computing power enable firms to extract more information
from larger datasets. We discuss how this technological shift affects finance jobs, and then
consider the wider implications for market efficiency, competition, conflicts of interest,
and financial stability. Part of our analysis focuses on firms that can be considered “early

adopters” of AI-powered trading such as proprietary trading firms and quantitative asset
managers.
AI is a potential source for efficiency gains as more accurate forecasts can bring prices
closer to their “fundamental value” and thereby improve valuations, the screening of
investment projects, and ultimately the allocation of capital in the economy. However, it
is not guaranteed that these benefits will materialize. Instead, investors may use AI to
generate information that is privately profitable, but socially unproductive—for example,
fast trading on public information releases or the identification of others’ trading patterns
in order to anticipate their behaviour. In the extreme, such strategies can even weaken the
incentives to produce socially useful information, and thus undermine market efficiency.
AI is also likely to reshape competition in financial markets. The large investments
required in data, computing, and model development create economies of scale, with the
potential to increase market concentration and limit competition. However, AI could also
lower barriers to entry and spur the entry of new firms. Consistent with these opposing
economic forces, a descriptive analysis of Dutch supervisory paints an overall mixed pic-
ture for the case of proprietary trading firms. Finally, we discuss how autonomous pricing
algorithms can help sustain noncompetitive outcomes, and argue that their unpredictable
behaviour complicates policy intervention.
The paper also discusses how AI may shape key financial market frictions such as
adverse selection and moral hazard. We highlight the channels through which AI may
either amplify existing information asymmetries or level the playing field. Further, we
discuss how AI can reshape existing agency conflicts such as those between employers and
employees, and give rise to new ones that arise from model complexity. We also stress
the underlying regulatory challenges.

1 Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming the financial industry.1 Financial in-
termediaries (e.g., asset managers and banks) are investing heavily in AI capabilities,
including large-scale data infrastructures, machine learning models, computing resources,
and human capital. Recent surveys (e.g., ESMA, 2026) indicate that AI adoption is
widespread and accelerating across financial intermediaries, with particularly rapid diffu-
sion among large firms and those operating in securities markets. Moreover, according to
several recent studies, occupations in the financial industry are among those most exposed
to AI-driven automation.2
The importance of AI for finance is not surprising given the central role of informa-
tion in virtually every financial activity. Financial intermediaries exist largely to collect,
process, and transmit information in order to allocate capital, manage risks, and miti-
gate informational frictions between market participants (Levine,1997). By combining
vast amounts of data with increasingly powerful algorithms and computing resources, AI
potentially enables financial intermediaries to generate more accurate predictions, make
better decisions, and ultimately allocate capital more efficiently at a lower cost.
These developments raise fundamental questions for economists and policymakers.
Does AI improve the efficiency of financial markets by making prices more informative
about fundamentals and improving capital allocation? Or does it instead strengthen the
market power of a few technologically advanced intermediaries? Will AI reduce infor-
mational frictions by making financial information more accessible, or widen the infor-

The goal of this paper is to discuss these questions in light of recent academic research.
We organize our discussion around the idea that financial intermediaries combine data
and labor to produce information and make decisions, such as evaluating investment op-
portunities or selecting portfolios. AI transforms this production technology by increasing
the productivity of data in information production through advances in algorithms and
computing power. As a result, financial intermediaries can extract more information from
increasingly diverse datasets while relying less on labor-intensive information processing.
This perspective provides a useful framework for understanding the economic effects
of AI in finance. By changing the technology through which information is produced,
AI affects not only the demand for labor, but also the efficiency of financial markets,
the competitive structure of the financial industry, informational frictions among market
participants, and ultimately financial stability.
We first examine the implications of AI for the production of information and the
resulting implications for the role of labor in the financial industry (Section 2). We
then discuss how AI technologies are being deployed across different types of financial
intermediaries, with particular attention to proprietary trading firms and quantitative
asset managers, which have emerged as “early adopters” (Section3).
We then turn to the broader economic consequences of AI for financial markets (Sec-
tions 4 and 5). We argue that AI has the potential to generate substantial efficiency
gains, but at the same time may also amplify existing and create new distortions. In the
following, we highlight the key channels.

Efficiency and capital allocation. AI enables investors to form more accurate
forecasts of cash flows and asset returns at lower cost. This can lead to improved asset
valuations, better screening and monitoring of investment projects, and ultimately more
efficient capital allocation. However, these gains are not guaranteed. Financial interme-
diaries can instead use AI to produce information that is privately valuable but socially
wasteful. For example, AI can be used to trade rapidly on newly released information at
the expense of accuracy or to extract signals from the trading footprints of others rather
than from information about fundamentals. Such activities generate little social value
and may even reduce incentives for producing fundamental information. As a result, AI
could ultimately render asset prices less informative.
The nature of the underlying data also matters. Because AI relies heavily on historical
data, it may perform poorly in novel environments or when evaluating projects whose
payoffsmaterializeonlyoverlonghorizons. Asa result, AImayrejectgenuinelyinnovative
projects or distort long-term investment decisions. In either case, this could lead to missed
investment opportunities for society.
Market power and industry structure.AI has the potential to alter the com-
petitive landscape in financial markets. On the one hand, it may strengthen economies
of scale in data, computing, and model development and thereby increase concentration
and market power. On the other hand, it could also facilitate the creation of startups
and thereby increase competition, making this ultimately an empirical question. A brief
analysis for Dutch supervisory data for proprietary trading firms (PTFs) paints a mixed
picture, consistent with these opposing channels. While PTFs have gained market share
on aggregate, within-sector concentration has remained rather flat, or even decreased.
We also discuss how AI enables autonomous pricing algorithms that can sustain non-

competitive outcomes, intentionally or inadvertently, and thereby weakens competition
in financial markets. Moreover, as predicting the behavior of autonomous algorithms
is difficult, the adoption of such algorithms for securities trading can complicate policy
interventions.
Informational frictions.AI can either reduce or amplify informational frictions.
While it can make information more widely available, it can also increase the informa-

and trading frictions.
Financial stability.Finally, we discuss how these developments may affect financial
stability. The widespread use of similar datasets and data-processing algorithms could
increase the correlation in investors’ signals, give rise to “crowded trades”, and amplify
fire sales in times of distress. At the same time, incentives for acquiring signals not
yet reflected in prices can act as a countervailing force. While greater concentration
among financial intermediaries, resulting from the economies of scale discussed above,
may further amplify these risks, it can also give rise to more prudent behavior due to
“skin in the game”. We also discuss other financial stability aspects such as malicious
behavior, model risk, and changes to the nature of financial intermediation.

2 New Inputs: Data, Computing, and Algorithms
2.1 Producing financial information
The financial industry produces and facilitates the trading of financial contracts to share
risks, transfer resources, and provide incentives (Philippon,2015). Information is a key
input in this process. As emphasized byLevine (1997), financial markets and institutions
arise to mitigate the frictions created by imperfect information. Information enables
investors to assess the payoffs of financial contracts more accurately and make better
investment decisions. It also reduces frictions arising from informational asymmetries,
such as adverse selection and moral hazard.
Many finance jobs are therefore related to data collection and processing, enabling
professionals to make predictions and act accordingly. For instance, securities analysts
collect and process data to forecast firms’ future cash flows, estimate their “fair” values,
and make buy/sell recommendations. Similarly, active fund managers collect data about
securities to forecast their returns and make buy/sell decisions based on these forecasts.
People in these occupations transform data into knowledge, i.e., information that can

decisions. For instance, with more data, an active asset manager can identify undervalued

capitalist: more knowledge about the prospects of potential investment targets enables
the venture capitalist to distinguish good from bad projects better and allocate capital
accordingly.

2.2 Data
Data have always played a central role in finance. Traditionally, finance professionals have
relied on (i) firms’ reports (e.g., regulatory filings, accounting data, etc.), (ii) macroeco-
nomic data, (iii) market data (e.g., stock returns, order flows, quotes, etc.), and (iv) data
obtained by intermediaries (e.g., banks and broker-dealers) about their clients as sources
of information.
What is novel is the sharp increase in the volume and diversity of data available for
forecasting future cash flows or returns. These data are often referred to as “alternative
data” because they provide an alternative to the traditional sources mentioned above.
Most alternative data can be grouped into one of three broad categories: (i) Data gen-
erated as a by-product of consumers’ decisions (e.g., internet traffic on a firm’s website,
point-of-sale data, credit card data); (ii) Data generated through communications on so-
cial media (e.g., messages on StockTwits or X); (iii) Sensor data (e.g., satellite imagery
or geolocation data).
Alternative data are often unstructured (e.g., text, images, or audio), and therefore
often collected, cleaned and processed by data vendors before reselling them in a format
suitable for analysis. The number of such providers has grown rapidly alongside the
digitalization of the economy. For example, Dessaintet al. (2024) document an increase
in the number of providers specializing in “finance” datasets from about 100 in 2000 to
more than 500 in 2017 (seeFigure 1). In addition, new data intermediaries such as Eagle
Alpha, Databricks, and Datarade serve as marketplaces connecting buyers and sellers of
alternative data. For instance, Datarade was listing over 3,500 alternative datasets as of
2025.

2.3 Algorithms
Algorithms play a dual role: they improve the generation of knowledge by raising the
productivity of data relative to labor, and they facilitate the use of this knowledge as
input in financial decisions. In the first role, algorithms increase the quality and quantity
of knowledge that can be produced from a given combination of data and labor; this is
captured bya in the production function (1), where the elasticityα(c,a) of knowledge
with respect to data also depends on computing power c. In the second role, firms
develop and train an additional layer of algorithms that maps knowledgeKi, together
with other inputs, into decisions such as the implementation of trading strategies or
portfolio selection. In both roles, machine learning (ML) provides the methods used to
develop and improve these algorithms. A single AI system may perform both roles. For
instance, an agent based on a large language model (LLM) can extract information from
filings and then act on it directly
For decades, algorithms have been central to the implementation of quantitative meth-
ods for forecasting and decision-making in financial markets. The large-scale deployment
of data-driven models dates back to at least the 1980s, which coincides with the founding
of firms such as Renaissance Technologies and D.E. Shaw (see Section3.2 for details).
Similarly, algorithmic trading has become prevalent over the past two decades and ac-
counts for the majority of trading volume across many asset classes (equities, futures,
options, and FX). For example, for Dutch blue-chip stocks, Figure2 shows that algorith-
mic trading accounts for approximately 70% of total trading volume by value and an even
larger share by number of trades. Algorithmic trading provides a natural technological
infrastructure for deploying AI applications, but an order classified as algorithmic does
not necessarily involve ML.

Arguably, the modern era of AI began withKrizhevsky et al. (2012), who show that

visual recognition task, demonstrating that deep learning is practical at scale. The ML
algorithms that have since been adopted in finance are novel in both roles. In the first,
they can handle a large number of predictors relative to the number of observations
(Section 2.3.2). In the second, they can learn how to make autonomous decisions without
relying solely on pre-set rules (Section2.3.3).
2.3.1 Artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms
While AI refers an entire machine-based system (recall the definition in footnote 1), ML
encompasses methods that use data or interactive feedback to improve predictions and de-
ECB Discussion Paper Series No 32
17

cisions.8 For predictive applications, an algorithm learns statistical relationships that map
inputs into forecasts, classifications, or representations, typically in high-dimensional set-
tings where regularization methods are used to improve out-of-sample performance. For
decision-making, an algorithm learns a policy that maps information about the current
state into an action, taking into account the rewards generated by that action. This dis-
tinction matters because predictive learning generally treats the surrounding environment
as given, whereas decision-making frequently occurs in an environment that is at least in
part shaped by the algorithm’s own actions. In financial markets, individual actions affect
the environment and the behavior of other participants, so the algorithm must continue
to learn and update in tandem with changes in the market environment. For example,
state-of-the-art algorithms for executing large orders in electronic markets are designed
to learn how the market reacts to the execution algorithm’s own actions.
In discussing ML algorithms, it is useful to distinguish a model’slearning paradigm
from its architecture. The learning paradigm describes what the model learns from and
what it is trained to optimize; Box 2 presents the three main categories, namely supervised
learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning (RL). On the other hand, the
architecture describes the functional structure through which inputs are represented and
processed. The same architecture can be trained under different learning paradigms, and
the same learning paradigm can be implemented using different architectures.

Box 2: Learning paradigms: How a model learns
1.Supervised learninguses data that include pairs of inputs and corresponding
labels, and the model learns direct mappings from the inputs to labels by
minimizing some error function. This paradigm is suited to classification
and regression tasks with clearly defined labels. A financial application is
forecasting future corporate earnings, cash flows, or asset returns.
2.Unsupervised learningrelies on data without labeled outputs. Rather than
predicting predefined targets, the model identifies hidden structures or statis-
tical regularities in the data. Typical tasks include clustering, which groups
observations with similar characteristics, and dimensionality reduction, which
summarizes high-dimensional data while retaining key information. In fi-
nance, unsupervised learning can be used to extract latent factors from large
datasets, cluster firms or investors into groups, or reduce a large collection of
predictors to a smaller set of informative representations, which is a clear ex-
ample of how raw data is used to produce useful knowledge; see, e.g.,Cartea
et al. (2025b).
3. Reinforcement learning trains models through interaction with an environ-
ment, using feedback in the form of rewards or penalties associated with
model actions. The objective is to maximize cumulative rewards over time,
which makes this paradigm particularly well-suited to sequential and dynamic
decision-making problems (Sutton and Barto,1998). RL in finance can be
used for optimal trade execution, where a large order is strategically split
into smaller pieces and executed over time to minimize costs or market im-
ECB Discussion Paper Series No 32
19

pact (Carteaet al., 2015).a Because the actions selected by an RL agent can
affect its future environment, RL is particularly consequential when several
algorithms interact strategically.
These paradigms serve different purposes, and they typically map onto the two roles
of algorithms. Supervised and unsupervised learning produce knowledge from data,
for example by forecasting stock returns or extracting latent factors. RL selects
actions and learns from the rewards they generate, for example when an agent learns
how to execute a large order. However, the distinction is not absolute because
modern AI systems are often built as pipelines that combine several paradigms
at different stages of development or deployment, rather than relying on a single
learning method throughout.
aRL does not necessarily entail online learning. Models are often trained offline, for example
in simulated environments, and deployed without further updating. The RL framework can also
describe decision-making at a higher level of aggregation. A firm that selects which algorithm
to deploy in each state of the market, and updates this choice based on realized immediate and
future rewards, is effectively implementing an RL policy over algorithms.
Model architectures define the set of relationships that a model can represent and the
degree of flexibility with which it can approximate patterns in the data. Several model
architectures are commonly used in financial applications. Linear models are relatively
simple and interpretable, and are popular in financial domains where speed and explain-
ability are important. Tree-based models, including decision trees, random forests, and
gradient boosting methods, can learn nonlinear relationships and retain a partially inter-
pretable form, and are used in, for example, credit scoring (Dastileet al., 2020). Neural
network architectures are a flexible class of function approximators, but are generally
considered less interpretable than linear models.

These distinctions matter from a policy perspective because they give rise to different
regulatory considerations. Model architectures largely determine the range and complex-
ity of patterns that can be extracted from data, the associated requirements for compute
and specialized labor, and the difficulty of interpreting outputs (Dixonet al., 2020). By
contrast, learning paradigms determine whether AI is used primarily to generate predic-
tions or to make decisions through feedback, and concerns about unintended or emergent
outcomes are usually associated with the paradigm rather than the architecture.
2.3.2 Making predictions
One key strength of ML algorithms relative to more traditional predictive tools (e.g.,
OLS regressions) is their ability to handle a very large number of predictors relative
to the number of observations, a property referred to as “complexity” byKelly et al.
(2024). This makes them particularly well-suited to harness the informational content of
large and unstructured data, which often characterize the alternative datasets discussed
in Section 2.2. However, the marginal value of data and of model complexity is not
unbounded. In terms of (1), diminishing returns would requireα(c,a) to be less than
one. Moreover, greater algorithmic sophistication (highera) does not always yield more
knowledge. Cartea et al. (2025c) show that an increase in model complexity is not always
optimal when model features are observed with noise. Specifically, they show that the
marginal benefits of increasing model complexity will, at some point, start to diminish
and become negative as the size of the model increases due to measurement noise in the
learned parameters used by the complex model.9
Many academic studies show that ML algorithms improve forecasting power relative to

non-linear relationships and their ability to handle a far larger number of predictors than
traditional methods.
For similar reasons, several studies document the superiority of ML algorithms in
predicting firms’ earnings. For instance, Cao and You (2024) compare the predictive
performance of decision trees and neural networks for corporate earnings for U.S. firms
from1975to2019to the performanceofstandardlinearforecastingmodels(e.g., arandom
walk model or an AR(1) model). They train ML algorithms to predict yearly earnings one,
two, and three years ahead using 60 features obtained from firms’ financial statements
(e.g., balance sheets or income statements), and observe that ML models achieve higher
out-of-sample forecasting accuracy than simpler models.
These improvements in out-of-sample forecasting performance do not arise from algo-
rithms alone; they depend on complementary investments in data, computing resources,
and specialized human capital. Because acquiring and developing these inputs may in-
volve substantial fixed costs, access to the most sophisticated predictive models may differ
across financial intermediaries.

a deep learning algorithm to select profitable investment strategies directly instead of
simply forecasting returns. Empirically, their algorithm, which does not rely on human
decision-making, generates an impressive alpha of 13%.

3.2 Asset management
The asset management sector is diverse, spanning mutual and hedge funds, sovereign
wealth funds, family offices, and insurance companies. Moreover, a significant share of
the sector outside the United States is integrated into universal banks (Ferreiraet al.,
2018). Given that these tend to be large organizations with substantial headcount, there
is a diverse range of AI use cases, ranging from investment-related tasks (e.g., exploration
of new asset classes, extraction of trading signals from large/complex datasets, portfo-
lio optimization and asset allocation) to back-office functions (document management,
workflow optimization) and customer-facing activities (robo advising, marketing).
Matters are more clear-cut for quantitatively focused asset managers. Given limited
organizational overhead, they are poised to devote most AI-related efforts to return pre-
diction and trade execution. While the origins of quantitative investing can be traced back
to the development of basic finance models (1950s to 1970s), the industry only began to
reach scale in the 1980s as the use of models became practical with advances in comput-
ing power and increased data availability. While early quantitative approaches were often
“structural” (i.e., grounded in theory), this period also laid the foundation for a more
data-driven approach spearheaded by hedge funds such as Renaissance Technologies (see
Box 4) or D.E. Shaw, largely run by mathematicians and computer scientists. Since then,
quantitative investing has grown tremendously, and spilled over from institutional into
retail products.30

resource allocation, or whether it introduces costs, externalities, and risks that could
reduce social welfare.34

to new information, make prices more informationally efficient in the sense that prices

AI-powered intermediaries can enhance price informativeness.

4.2 Market power
AI is likely to change the competitive environment in which financial firms operate. The
underlying mechanisms can be different, depending on whether AI is used to improve
forecasting or for the automation of decision-making.
4.2.1 Improvement in forecasting ability
The increased use of data in the economy as a whole seems to have led to higher market
concentration. Farboodi et al. (2019) study a model where firms accumulate data about
their customers, which helps them to produce better products and increase sales. This
gives rise to a positive feedback loop between data and sales, so that economic activity
becomes concentrated among a few large firms. Furthermore,Begenau et al. (2018) show
that it is easier for financial markets to assess the value of data-rich companies, which
lowers their cost of capital and helps to further fuel their growth.Babina et al. (2024)
show that the same dynamics remain true with the adoption of AI specifically over the
period 2010-2018.
However, these general mechanisms are not directly applicable to financial markets
because transaction data are typically collected and sold by exchanges to other market
participants. Thisimpliesthatentrantsandsmallerfirmscaneasilyaccessvastamountsof

4.2.2 Autonomous decision-making
AI-powered algorithms for autonomous trading may directly affect competition by pro-
foundly changing the strategic interaction between market participants. A growing lit-
erature studies the interaction between price-setting algorithms and the implications for
the nature of competition.
One strand of the literature shows that simple algorithms can coordinate on collusive
equilibria that human agents would find difficult to reach (Calvanoet al., 2020), with
the risk that ever-more sophisticated algorithms may be able to reach such outcomes
in complex real-world environments (seeKeppo et al. (2025) for recent experiments with
LLMs of different sophistication). More generally,Cartea et al. (2026b) show that learning
algorithmscanachieveanypayoffprofilethatisfeasibleandindividuallyrational, meaning
that algorithms can learn to collude where the collusive outcome is sustained by a reward-
punishment mechanism.
Another strand of the literature points out that, to the contrary, the lack of sophisti-
cation of algorithms can lead them to “mistakenly” coordinate on noncompetitive prices
(see, e.g., Asker et al. (2024), Abada et al. (2024), and Zhao and Xu(2024)). In fact,
even simple and reasonable design choices for algorithms such as reacting to competitors’
price changes are sufficient to prevent competition from working as expected (Brown and
MacKay (2023), Wang et al. (2022)).
Importantly, in many of these environments it may be a best response for a firm to
keep a simple algorithm leading to a noncompetitive outcome, rather than using a more
sophisticated one that would eventually lead to lower prices. Thus, both sophisticated
pricing algorithms (via sophisticated collusive strategies) and naive algorithms (via lack
ECB Discussion Paper Series No 32
50

of competitive aggressiveness) may ultimately lead to less competitive prices.41 Empiri-
cally, Assadet al.(2024) provide clean causal evidence that the introduction of pricing
algorithms in the German retail gasoline market led to higher prices.
Some recent papers study the interaction of algorithms in financial markets.Colliard
et al. (2026) run experiments with simple RL algorithms in a market-making game. They
find that the algorithms only partially learn to compete despite playing for many rounds,
and do not reach competitive spreads. While this is generally in line with the “lack of
sophistication” view, the authors suggest that financial markets may be special due to the
strengthofadverseselectionandnoisypayoffs. Intuitively, noiseleadstomorecompetitive
outcomes by making it more difficult for algorithms to learn to undercut their competitors,
whereas adverse selection reduces noise and thus leads to more competitive outcomes.
Dou et al. (2023) study the complementary case of algorithms used by informed in-
vestors. They show that these algorithms can learn collusive strategies when there is
little noise in the market, or on the contrary may behave noncompetitively by mistake
when there is too much noise. In general, it seems that outcomes crucially depend on the
sophistication of algorithms relative to the complexity of the environment, which varies
across assets and over time. Another approach to study the role of sophistication is to
compare how different families of algorithms fare in the same environment, as inCartea
et al. (2022a) in a market-making context.42

and understanding the long-run behavior of even relatively simple RL algorithms is dif-

4.3 AI and informational rents
4.3.1 Adverse selection costs
Asymmetric information is a key friction in financial markets. For instance, a hedge fund
might have private information about a stock’s incoming earnings announcement, which
may induce it to buy or to sell the stock. In these situations, the profits of the informed
party are obtained at the expense of the less informed party and are therefore a cost
for the latter (adverse selection costs). To cover these costs, less informed investors are
willing to buy (sell) assets only at a discount (markup). This phenomenon can explain
why secondary markets are not perfectly liquid, why assets may be underpriced in primary
markets, and why markets may break down.
The effect of AI on asymmetric information and adverse selection costs is ambigu-
ous. On the one hand, AI could enable less informed investors to catch up with more
informed investors, thereby reducing adverse selection costs. On the other hand, rather
than leveling the playing field, AI can aggravate information asymmetries by increasing
the informational edge of investors who make significant investments in datasets, algo-
rithms, and computing power.
For example, the SEC’s introduction of EDGAR in 1993 lowered the cost of accessing
regulatory filings by requiring U.S. public companies to submit their information elec-
tronically to an online repository.Gomez (2024) shows that this reduced informational
asymmetries between firm’s managers and investors.43 However, he also finds an increase
in information asymmetries between sophisticated and unsophisticated investors. One
possible reason is that sophisticated investors have greater information processing pos-

particularly useful in the financial industry because moral hazard is an important friction.

employees are working (Jiang et al., 2025), or indirectly, by comparing an employee’s
forecast or performance to that generated by an AI tool. For instance, fund manager
performance can be benchmarked against the performance of an AI agent.45

prices and the incentive to acquire information. It is well known that the economic value
of informative signals dissipates quickly as they become available to a larger number
of agents and are reflected instantaneously in prices (e.g.,Grossman and Stiglitz,1980).
This, in turn, creates incentives to look for other signals not yet reflected in market prices.
Accordingly, market participants will find it profitable to invest in different (i.e. propri-
etary) models and acquire alternative datasets. The underlying mechanism is consistent
with the model ofWagner (2011), where commonality in exposures gives rise to higher
liquidation risk. Accordingly, investors aim to choose portfolios that differ from those of
others, and optimally forego diversification benefits.
5.2 Competition and market concentration
As discussed previously, AI may increase market concentration in certain market seg-
ments such as market making and (statistical) arbitrage. This can affect financial sta-
bility through two channels, namely the rise of systemically important players and the
competition-stability trade-off. While these aspects are not new, these channels may be-
come amplified through the economies of scale inherent in the use of AI models in financial
markets (see Section4.2).
First, consider the issue of systemic relevance. Typically, the “too-big-to-fail” problem
centers around banks due to their central role for retail deposits, credit provision and
payments. However, there were several instances in modern history (most notably LTCM
in1998, butmorerecentlyLehmanBrothersin2008, KnightCapitalin2012, andArchegos
in 2021) where large trading firms, and especially hedge funds, were perceived as systemic
and became subject to a coordinated bailout/rescue. The concern typically centers on
the indirect contagion effects resulting from large exposures and leverage. While these
ECB Discussion Paper Series No 32
60

concerns continue to apply, the AI-powered increase in the size (and scope) of PTFs may
turn them into new systemic players. Since some of these firms are the main intermediaries
in key markets (e.g., equities, futures, FX), their collapse could lead to a significant
impairment of market functioning (see alsoYadav and Brogaard,2026).
Indeed, empirical evidence suggests that the failure of a major intermediary can create
a “capacity gap” that cannot be filled instantaneously, but rather takes time to close.
Bogousslavsky et al. (2021) study the 2012 failure of Knight Capital, a large market maker
in U.S. equities (see Box 6 for details).49 They show that the resulting decline in market
liquidity was significant, especially for large institutions (as measured by implementation
shortfall). However, the overall fallout of this episode was relatively contained as the
firm was able to raise new capital within a week and was ultimately taken over by a
competitor. In particular, the latter point suggests that a competitive ecosystem can
be helpful in containing bankruptcy costs. However, this may become more difficult if
economies of scale lead to increased market concentration.

5.3 Malicious behavior
While AI is a potential source of efficiency, it can equally be used for harmful activity.
Interestingly, as explained previously (see Sections4.3.2 and 4.2.2), this need not even be
intentional.
One major concern is the use of AI for “disinformation”, i.e., the generation and
subsequent propagation of false information. This is, of course, not a new phenomenon; an
early well-known case is the “Great Stock Exchange Fraud of 1814”.51 However, the advent
of GenAI means that the costs of this form of market manipulation have fallen sharply.
Combined with market participants’ incentives for engaging in the use of alternative data
(e.g., social media), this creates a perfect storm that may adversely affect markets.
One case in point is a 2023 deepfake of an explosion at the U.S. Pentagon, which
subsequently went viral on social media and had a short-lived but significant effect on the
U.S. stock market.52 The ability to use sophisticated AI models to generate new content
may thus further amplify the mechanism explored inDugast and Foucault(2018). As data

5.4 Model and data risks
The complex nature of AI models makes it difficult to validate their outputs. This is
particularly true for LLMs because their probabilistic nature favors statistical plausibility
over factual correctness or logic. This can lead to “hallucinations”, i.e., factually incorrect
outputs that appear credible to the observer. Accordingly, this feature can jeopardize
effective (internal and regulatory) model oversight and risk controls, and lead to erroneous
(investment) decisions.53 Importantly, such issues become more rampant when relying on
pre-trained models from third parties. While trading firms face legal obligations54 aimed
at ensuring model oversight (e.g., by employing qualified staff), including in the case of

5.5 The nature of intermediation
In the aftermath of the 2007-08 financial crisis, regulators moved to curtail banks’ propri-
etary trading activities (e.g., via the Volcker Rule or the supplementary leverage ratio).
While these measures have likely contributed to making the financial system safer, it has
been shown that they have also limited banks’ ability to act as intermediaries in financial
markets (Adrian et al., 2017a; Cenedese et al., 2021; Breckenfelder and Ivashina, 2021).
Since PTFs and hedge funds have expanded to fill the void, this raises the question of
whether the nature of liquidity provision has changed, and how this will be affected by the
rise of AI-powered trading. For example, PTFs have historically aimed to intermediate
by relying on technology, not balance sheet capacity. Together with a focus on less
noisy short-term predictions and the minimization of clearing and settlement costs, PTFs
typically end the trading day flat. Accordingly, some economists have questioned whether
the contribution of PTFs to market liquidity is meaningful (e.g.,Haldane, 2012; Stiglitz,
2014).

6 Conclusion
We argue in this paper that AI can be understood as a technological shock that improves
financial intermediaries’ ability to leverage data for making better predictions and, ulti-
mately, decisions. Framed this way, AI’s consequences extend well beyond automating
existing tasks: it reshapes the demand for labor, the competitive structure of the securities
industry, the informativeness of prices, agency conflicts, and the stability of the financial
system. Our discussion suggests that the ultimate impact of AI on financial markets is
not a foregone conclusion in either direction. On the positive side, AI may make markets
more efficient, help level the playing field, and ameliorate a variety of frictions. At the
same time, it may also tilt market participants towards socially unproductive (or outright
harmful) activities, give rise to market power and algorithmic collusion, create new forms
of moral hazard, and jeopardize financial stability.

Our synthesis highlights several interesting avenues for future research. We highlight
three examples. First, a significant body of research studies how technology improves pre-
dictions and the resulting implications for financial markets. By contrast, research on the
effects of automated decision-making is naturally more limited due to its nascent nature.
This is likely to prove as a fruitful area of research going forward. Second, the industrial
organization of financial markets deserves more attention against the background of the
mentioned ambiguous effects of AI on market concentration and competition. Potential
challenges for pushing this agenda will be the availability of granular data and the de-
velopment of suitable empirical methods. Third, further research could study financial
stability issues, such as an analysis of the risks of “model and data monoculture” and
their role for the amplification of shocks.

A MeasuringAIIntensityfromWeb-scrapedJobPost-

This appendix describes the data and methodology used to measure the adoption of AI by
PTFs, multi-manager hedge funds (“pod shops”), banks, and asset managers, as presented
in Figures4 and 5.
A.1 Data collection
We collect publicly available job postings from the career pages of 35 financial institutions,
spanning four categories: 14 PTFs, 5 pod shops, 9 banks, and 7 asset managers (see
Table A1 for the full list). For 2025, job postings were scraped directly from each firm’s
career website in November 2025. For PTFs, we additional retrieved archived versions of
the same career pages from the Wayback Machine (https://web.archive.org) for the
years 2022-2024 whenever possible. Not all firms have data available for every year; the
coverage for each firm depends on whether the career page of interest was captured and
preserved by the Wayback Machine at the relevant date.
Each job posting is compiled into a standardized record containing the job title, re-
quired skills, and described responsibilities. We apply a cue-word-based language filter to
retain only English-language postings, thereby ensuring consistency in the text classifica-
tion procedure described below.

This appendix describes the data and methodology used to measure PTF activity. Our
sample of PTFs consists of firms that are members of either FIA EPTA or APT, two
industry associations representing leading principal trading firms.
B.1 Share of trading
We measure the share of trading volume by PTFs on a quarterly basis over the period
2020–2026. We focus on four asset classes: futures, stocks, options, and exchange-traded
funds (ETFs).
