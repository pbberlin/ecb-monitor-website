1 Introduction
Dear Ladies and Gentlemen,
It is a pleasure to be with you in Munich today. Over the years, this conference has witnessed many
shifts in the global economy. We are living through another profound transition – and that is what I
would like to talk about today.
Let me start with the big picture. For a long time, the United States was a driving force behind the
rules-based trading order. And China was the world’s factory – supplying goods to the rest of the
world at highly competitive prices.
But the balance of economic power has shifted. Economic relations are being increasingly shaped by
strategic considerations, particularly in sectors such as technology. Trade ﬂows, too, are shifting
along geopolitical lines. And that shift is far from over. Its consequences go beyond trade. They are
shaping the environment in which monetary policy operates.
So how can Europe navigate these new realities? Two questions will guide me today. First: how can
Europe strengthen its economic resilience without giving up the beneﬁts of open markets and rules-
based cooperation? And second: how does monetary policy in the euro area respond to a world that
has become more volatile?
Let me begin with the forces behind these changes.

2 What do the geoeconomic realities look like?
Two developments in particular are reshaping global economic relations. The ﬁrst is China's changing
role in the global economy. For decades, China’s rise helped drive global economic integration. It also
tipped the balance of economic power, contributing to new rivalries and trade tensions.
China is still the world’s factory. But it has become something more than that as well: a major com‐
petitor in advanced technologies, competing increasingly on quality and innovation, as well as price.
And its dominant position in certain critical inputs gives it another source of political leverage: the
ability to restrict exports.
China's export controls on rare earths in 2025 showed how such dependencies can be used to exert
pressure. China reﬁnes around 90% of the rare earth elements used in permanent magnets – essen‐
tial components in electric vehicles, wind turbines and defence systems.  European manufacturers
felt the impact of those restrictions, too – even though Europe was not the intended target.
The second development is the shift in US trade policy. The United States has powerful levers of its
own – its market, its currency, its central role in global ﬁnance. And it is willing to use trade restric‐
tions to pursue strategic objectives. According to our estimates, the average US tariﬀ rate has risen
from 2.3% in 2024 to almost 12% – a level last seen in the early 1940s.
China’s export controls and the move in US trade policy illustrate a broader trend: the boundaries
between trade policy, industrial policy and security policy are blurring.
Global trade has held up better than many feared. But trade patterns are changing. Our analysis
shows that trade between geopolitical blocs has been losing ground to trade within those blocs since
2018 – dropping by around 30% compared with historical patterns.
For European businesses, the growing risk of politically motivated supply disruptions is tipping the
balance between eﬃciency and resilience. Not every dependency is a vulnerability. And not every
eﬀort to reduce dependency is worth its cost. So the question is not whether to diversify – but where.
3 How Europe can hold its own
I won’t need to tell an audience like yourselves how exposed Germany is to all of this. Let me give you
just one ﬁgure: the two countries at the centre of this rivalry – China and the US – take roughly one
out of every seven euro of our goods exports.
Our own analysis tells a clear story: a more fragmented world economy could take a comparatively
heavy toll on Europe.  But it’s not as if Europe doesn’t have weight of its own. The question is how to
use it. I would focus on four priorities. Let me talk you through them in turn.
3.1 Reducing critical dependencies
The ﬁrst priority is to reduce critical dependencies – and to do so selectively, not across the board.
Each of you manages supply risks for your own company, and you have every incentive to do it well.
But no single company can manage the macroeconomic risk. 
Critical inputs make up a small share of what we import. But when they stop coming, the damage is
out of all proportion.  A shortage at one point in a supply chain can disrupt production far beyond it.
That may justify a targeted role for the state – alongside ﬁrms’ own eﬀorts to diversify, rather than
instead of them.

EU Trade Commissioner Maroš Šefčovič put it this way: “openness without security is vulnerability”.
Security and openness are not opposites. Europe needs both. 
The EU has instruments to address these risks, from the Critical Raw Materials Act to plans for joint
stockpiling of rare earths.  These instruments should address clearly identiﬁed vulnerabilities, rather
than becoming a pretext for broader protectionism. So: de-risk where the dependency is clearly crit‐
ical. And stay open everywhere else.
3.2 Strengthening the multilateral trading system
The second priority is to strengthen the multilateral trading system. For Europe, rules are worth more
than muscle. Despite growing tensions, 72% of world merchandise trade still takes place on WTO
most-favoured-nation terms.
That shows how much the system matters – above all, to the small and medium-sized economies that
depend on reliable rules.
Some WTO disciplines need to address trade distortions more eﬀectively – particularly those
involving opaque subsidies and state-linked ﬁrms. Progress will require the involvement of the major
players, including the United States and China.
At the same time, WTO agreements already allow targeted measures to be taken against dumping
and certain harmful subsidies, provided the conditions for their use are met. Europe should use
these instruments where justiﬁed. But it must also uphold the principles of openness and non-dis‐
crimination itself.
3.3 Broadening the network of trade agreements
The third priority is to broaden Europe’s network of trade agreements. The interim agreement with
Mercosur is being provisionally applied, negotiations with India and Australia have been concluded,
and the EU and Mexico have signed their modernised agreement.  Once all agreements are imple‐
mented, preferential agreements could cover more than half of the EU’s external trade – up from
around 46% at the end of 2025.
And the network also deepens with partners we already have: two weeks ago, the EU and Canada
agreed to work towards an ever-closer association – with a digital trade agreement as a ﬁrst concrete
step.
The value of these agreements goes beyond lower tariﬀs. They give European ﬁrms more markets to
sell to – and more suppliers to choose from when one door closes. But the beneﬁts will take time to
materialise. Our analysis suggests that the agreements with Mercosur and India will have only a
modest eﬀect on euro area trade in the short run.  And opening markets abroad is no substitute
for removing barriers at home. 
3.4 Deepening the single market
The fourth priority is to deepen the single market. This is where I see the greatest potential. Europe is
large on paper. It must become large in practice.
Even inside our single market, signiﬁcant barriers remain – particularly in services. One IMF study
estimates the costs of trading across EU borders as being equivalent to tariﬀs of around 44% on
goods and 110% on services.  Those are barriers we impose on ourselves. And they are ours to
remove.

Lifting them would help European ﬁrms achieve greater scale, become more productive, and
compete more eﬀectively – at home and abroad. Three areas illustrate what a more integrated
Europe could achieve: energy, capital markets and digital services.
First, energy. Nine Member States are still missing the 10% interconnection target that was set for
2020.  A better-connected European grid can help narrow price gaps between national markets,
make better use of renewables, and cushion supply shocks.
Second, capital markets. According to the Draghi report, Europe needs an additional 750 to 800
billion euro in investment every year up to 2030 to remain competitive.  Public funds alone cannot
meet that need. Europe must mobilise more private capital – and that requires deeper, more integ‐
rated ﬁnancial markets. Such markets would also help channel savings towards the most productive
investment opportunities across Europe.
There has been progress. Last month, the securitisation reform became the ﬁrst legislative initiative
under the Savings and Investments Union to reach a political agreement.  We also need a genuine
internal market for banking: more eﬃcient cross-border capital and liquidity management, accom‐
panied by appropriate safeguards.
The third area is digital services. Three non-European providers account for more than 70% of
Europe’s cloud market.  And Europe hosts an estimated 5% of the world’s AI computing capacity.
The United States accounts for almost 80%.
A deeper single market will not close these gaps on its own. But it would help European digital ﬁrms
scale across a market of 450 million people – and attract the investment they need. They also need
better conditions for private investment: reliable energy, faster permitting, and shorter waits for grid
connections.
Targeted public support may be justiﬁed where warranted by market failures or security risks. But
private investment will have to play the leading role. And that reinforces the case for deeper
European capital markets.
4 What this means for monetary policy
Let me turn to my second question: how does monetary policy respond to a world that has become
more volatile? Geopolitical developments have signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced the outlook for inﬂation in
recent years.
When we updated our strategy last year, we recognised that inﬂation could become more volatile,
with larger deviations from our 2% target – in both directions.
The Iran war and its repercussions for global energy markets illustrate this risk. Oil costs around a
third more than before the war began. European gas almost three times as much. And euro area
inﬂation, which stood at around 2% when the year began, is now at 4%.
Higher policy rates won’t produce more oil or gas.  But the longer the shock persists, the greater
the risk that higher energy prices will feed into price setting, wage bargaining and inﬂation expecta‐
tions.  That is what monetary policy can and must prevent: higher inﬂation becoming self-sustain‐
ing or even self-reinforcing.

So far, both underlying inﬂation and wage growth have been broadly stable, and longer-term expect‐
ations have remained close to 2%.  This shock is also composed diﬀerently than it was in 2022: it is
more concentrated on oil, there is no food or exchange-rate shock on top, and the labour market is
less tight.  Our analysis suggests that a repeat of 2022 is unlikely – as long as we do our job.
However, the dynamics of gas and electricity prices have also gained momentum in recent weeks.
And the early stages of the pricing chain point to upward pressures. Moreover, underlying inﬂation is
still elevated, and short-term inﬂation expectations have risen in line with the jump in headline inﬂa‐
tion. And every month of elevated inﬂation raises the risk of second-round eﬀects.
That’s why the Governing Council has responded cautiously, but ﬁrmly. We raised rates in June and
again in September. Two weeks ago, we held at 2.5%.  We’ll keep deciding meeting by meeting,
based on the data.  If the situation stays as it is, it seems highly probable that we’ll have to act
again.
Our strategy equips us for a volatile environment: alongside the baseline, we work systematically
with scenarios.  The September baseline had inﬂation peaking around the turn of the year and
staying above target well into 2027.  Since then, the outlook has deteriorated. Energy prices have
moved well above that path – and we are now approaching the adverse scenario of the September
projections. That is one reason why the Governing Council sees the risks to inﬂation on the upside.
So far, the economy has proved resilient. The euro area grew by 0.4% in the third quarter.
5 Conclusion
Let me conclude. Trade has become a lever of power. And this year has shown how fast geopolitics
travels: from a shipping lane to our price levels. That brings me back to the two questions I posed at
the beginning of my remarks today.
On the ﬁrst: Europe should pursue openness, resilience and competitiveness together. De-risk where
it’s critical. Strengthen the rules. Broaden the partnerships. But the biggest lever is at home: a single
market that is worthy of the name, in energy and in ﬁnance – and reforms that make Europe a better
place to invest. 
On the second: whatever the next shock, the Eurosystem has all the instruments it needs to deal with
more volatile inﬂation, and the resolve to use them. Our compass hasn’t changed. It points to a 2%
inﬂation rate over the medium term. And we’ll steer by it.
