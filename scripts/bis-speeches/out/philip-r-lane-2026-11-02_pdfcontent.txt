SPEECH
AI adoption in Europe
Keynote speech by Philip R. Lane, Member of the Executive Board
of the ECB, at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Economic
Research Symposium on AI and the economy
San Francisco, 30 October 2026
My aim today is to describe how AI is reshaping the euro area economy.[ ] Although the development of
frontier AI models is primarily taking place in the United States and China, the adoption of AI will be a
key determinant of European productivity growth in the coming years, while expenditure on AI adoption
also currently constitutes a significant stimulus to the euro area economy.[ ] In addition, European firms
are part of the global AI supply chain, while European investors are contributing to the funding of the
global buildout of AI infrastructure through equity and debt markets and the banking system.
I will also describe the key patterns we see in the data, with a primary focus on how the surveys
conducted by the ECB can provide insights into the AI adoption process and the implications for the
financial system.[ ][ ]
Aggregate trends
Digital investment in the euro area has expanded by 73 per cent in real terms since 2014, increasing
from 2.3 per cent to 3.3 per cent of GDP (Chart 1a). While digital investment has grown far more
quickly in the United States, the surge in digital investment (with an estimated clear acceleration since
2024) marks a distinct shift in the structure of the euro area economy.[ ] As shown in Chart 1b, in the
euro area digital investment has primarily been in intangible assets, while investment in data centres
has constituted a relatively small share.[ ]

In the ECB Corporate Telephone Survey, firms report that AI has added about 10 per cent to their
capital expenditure plans this year. AI-related demand has helped activity to hold up over the last two
years – as reflected by the contributions of the “computers and other electronics” and “information and
communication services” sectors to total market production (Chart 2a). As illustrated in Chart 2b,
Purchasing Managers’ Index (PMI) indicators show that output, employment and new orders have
developed considerably more favourably in the technology equipment subsector than in other
manufacturing subsectors. For services, the software subsector has performed better than other
services sectors in terms of output, albeit not in terms of employment and new orders.

AI has been the main driver of global goods trade over the last year (Chart 3a). European firms are
contributors to the global AI supply chain. Table 4 shows that AI-related exports and imports are
growing quickly in the euro area, especially in relation to core data centre goods, which contain directly
deployed hardware components related to AI compute, storage and networking capacity. Possibly
related to differences in composition, the increase in AI import prices in the euro area is more muted
than for the other major economic regions (Chart 3b). Chart 5 shows that, while AI-related price
increases have contributed materially to the upward shift in goods inflation, the impact on headline
inflation is limited owing to the relatively low weight of AI-exposed goods in the consumer price index
(13.3 per cent in the non-energy industrial goods subcomponent and 3.4 per cent of the HICP).

The AI boom has also led to large initial public offerings (IPOs), including the SpaceX IPO in the
second quarter of 2026. In this context, euro area investors have stepped up their acquisitions of US
equities, and at the same time they have lowered their purchases of other foreign equities (Chart 9b).
Overall, these developments, together with analysis of credit spreads and the book-cover ratios of euro
area corporate issuers and credit spreads, suggest only limited crowding-out up to now.[ ]
Finally, lending to AI-related firms has contributed substantially to aggregate credit growth. This is
evident in Chart 10, which shows that the ECB Broad Intermediate Gauge (ECB-BIG) index would have
indicated a tighter credit environment in the absence of AI-related lending. In response to a dedicated
question in the October 2026 bank lending survey on the significance and impact of AI adoption,
several banks indicated that they expect a gradual but sustained increase in loan demand to finance
digital infrastructure, particularly data centres. Banks also reported that their current and prospective AI-
related lending is more likely to target infrastructure providers rather than software developers or firms
adopting AI, with this focus expected to become more pronounced over the coming months.

While the earnings calls data cover publicly listed firms, our Survey on the Access to Finance of
Enterprises (SAFE) is a large-scale survey that covers both large firms and small and medium-sized
enterprises (SMEs).[ ] In the fourth quarter of 2025 most firms participating in the SAFE reported that
they were using AI: 64 per cent of firms said they were using it either experimentally or moderately,
while only 7 per cent of firms reported that their use was “significant” (Chart 13).[ ] In terms of the

research and development or expanding their range of products, while just 15 per cent cited reducing

The round of the SAFE survey conducted in the fourth quarter of 2025 also ran a field experiment to
test whether planned AI investment by a firm responds to perceived competitive pressure from
domestic peer firms and foreign peer firms.[ ] Chart 14a shows that firms underestimate the rate of AI
adoption by their peer firms, with most firms underestimating the share of competitors that have
invested in AI, some by as much as 50 percentage points.
The results of the field experiment indicate that providing information about the AI investment plans of
their peers makes firms change their beliefs. In the field experiment, half of the firms were randomly
told the true share of peers investing in AI, at home and abroad. These firms revised up their beliefs
about the share of peers investing in AI, by 4.3 percentage points for domestic competitors and 3.3
percentage points for foreign competitors.
Chart 14b shows a striking asymmetry: firms plan more AI investment when they expect more domestic
peers to invest, whereas beliefs about the AI investment plans of foreign firms have no effect. The
impact of domestic competition is sizeable: a 1 percentage point increase in the expected share of
domestic firms undertaking AI investment raises their own expected AI investment rate by about 0.6
percentage points. That is a marked increase, given an average expected AI investment rate of 8.3 per
cent.

with 26 per cent in 2024 (Chart 16a).[ ] Adoption rates are higher for younger and more educated
workers (Chart 16b).[ ]

Chart 17a reports the time saved by workers on a range of tasks. The median working user reports
saving three hours per week, or about 7.7 per cent of median working time.[ ] However, the distribution
is highly skewed: most users enjoy only moderate time savings, while a small number report very high
efficiency gains. Generating or debugging code yields the largest gains, at six hours per week, although
only around 15 per cent of workers use AI for this purpose. A similar pattern emerges for data analysis,
automation of routine tasks and creating audio or visual content. By contrast, research, information
gathering, writing and text editing are among the most frequently cited uses. Yet time savings reported
for these tasks are considerably lower than for more technical tasks such as coding.
In most professions, workers do not see perceived efficiency gains as a threat to their jobs (Chart 17b).
The occupations that have the highest adoption rates tend to save the most hours and have more
positive views of AI. For example, managers use AI the most, save the most time and ultimately have
the most positive perception of the technology; this places them in a good position to help steer AI
adoption in the workplace.[ ]

The financing of AI
In this section, I report on how the European financial system has responded to the funding needs
associated with AI-related activity, together with the impact on financial market pricing.[ ]
Chart 21 shows that there has been a step up in venture capital financing of AI, with AI now accounting
for the majority of new originations. At the same time, venture capital is only relevant for a small set of
firms: most firms rely on internal funding, while the most popular sources of external funding are bank
loans and leasing arrangements (Chart 22).[ ]

A comparison of the AI financing plans of firms within the same country, industry and size class
indicates that more tangible investment tilts financing towards external sources (Chart 23). The use of
external finance for AI appears to hinge on the tangibility of the underlying investment: assets that are
pledgeable or collateralisable attract external funding, whereas more intangible expenditures are
financed internally. Investments in hardware and data infrastructure typically require large upfront
capital outlays that exceed available internal cash flow while providing pledgeable collateral, prompting
firms to seek complementary external funding. In contrast, employee training represents a smaller,
more incremental and intangible expense that firms can more readily finance out of retained earnings.
Overall, the findings highlight the importance of collateral in securing external financing. This pattern
may also point to a potential financing gap for intangible investments, which are critical for building
long-term AI capabilities.

Turning to bond pricing, Chart 25a (left panel) shows that AI-exposed non-financial firms in the STOXX
Europe 600 exhibit lower option-adjusted spreads than their non-exposed counterparts, although this
pattern has reversed somewhat recently. In particular, both the median and interquartile range of
spreads for AI-exposed firms have shifted downward in most years of the sample period. Concurrently,
these firms have issued a larger share of bonds with maturities exceeding ten years, whereas non-
exposed issuers exhibit a greater concentration of bonds at intermediate maturities.
Checking for credit risk (Expected Default Frequency, leverage, profitability and size), bond
characteristics and bond fixed effects, AI exposure is estimated to correspond to around 5 basis points
tighter spreads (Chart 25a, right panel). This combination of lower spreads and longer maturities
suggests more favourable debt financing conditions for AI-exposed firms, since longer maturities would
typically be associated with higher spreads in the absence of such advantages. Taken together, these
results indicate a modest but robust relation between AI exposure and a lower market-implied cost of
debt financing.
In relation to the equity market, AI-exposed firms recorded higher median annual stock returns than
non-exposed firms in most years of the sample (Chart 25b, left panel). The upper part of the return
distribution is also generally shifted upwards, suggesting that AI exposure has been associated not only
with higher typical returns but also with some strong outperformers. AI-exposed firms also tend to trade
at higher price-earnings ratios, consistent with investors assigning higher valuations to their expected
future earnings and, potentially, with a longer-duration equity profile. The evidence also points to a
robust equity-return premium to AI exposure of around 1.3 percentage points per quarter (Chart 25b,
right panel). This finding does not appear to be driven by the technology sector, as excluding firms from
that sector leaves the estimates virtually unchanged, nor by sector-wide AI rallies, since the spread
differential persists when AI-exposed issuers are compared only with peers in the same sector and
quarter.[ ]24

Fast-advancing AI is widening the set of operational risks to which banks are exposed (Chart 27b). The
rapid advancement of AI models is creating new cybersecurity challenges for banks by exposing
previously hidden vulnerabilities. While it is difficult to elicit the share of threats that is exclusively
related to the use of AI models, IT, cybersecurity and related incidents are already rising, accounting for
10-12 per cent of all operational risk events in 2025, nearly double their incidence in 2018. Recent
evidence shows that cyberattacks affecting payment systems can prompt banks to tap their liquidity
reserves or increase central bank borrowing. Cyberattacks on small banks can also disrupt local
banking markets, as depositors and customers may shift activity away from affected institutions. This
highlights how cyber risk can amplify liquidity and funding pressures, particularly for banks with limited
cyber resources. Cyber risk is only a part of a broader set of operational risks that banks are currently
facing.
In general, losses from these risks remain contained, amounting to 0.9 per cent of aggregate capital in
2025. However, this masks substantial heterogeneity in the distribution of losses across significant
institutions, with losses concentrated in tail events which may have repercussions for bank valuations,
earnings expectations and, when large or interconnected institutions are hit, systemic risk.

Conclusions
This speech has highlighted that AI is currently a significant contributor to growth in the euro area,
driven by both expenditure on AI adoption and the role of European firms in the global AI supply chain.
Surveys of firms and households indicate significant AI adoption rates, albeit unevenly distributed
across sectors and demographic groups. A striking pattern is that European households are net positive
about the employment impact of AI. However, the AI adoption process is still at a relatively early stage
and the extent and timing of the economy-wide transformative potential of AI remain highly uncertain.
The European financial system has responded to the external financing needs generated by increased
AI activity, even if internal funding remains the most important source of funding. European investors
are also major investors in foreign AI equities and the euro-denominated bond issuances by American
hyperscalers, even if the latter are primarily funded by a lower allocation to US Treasury bonds.
Looking ahead, tracking the near-term and medium-term implications of AI will remain high on the
analytical agenda at the ECB.

per cent shock is broadly in line with the 32.6 per cent gain in software-engineering productivity
between November 2022 and December 2025 that can be inferred from stock prices (Blumenfeld, A.,
Hazell, J., Lian, C. and Schaab, A. (2026), “
