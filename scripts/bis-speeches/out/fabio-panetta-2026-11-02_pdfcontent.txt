Saving means making a pact with the future: consuming less today to build 
prosperity for tomorrow. This pact only holds if savings can be channelled into 
investments that generate growth and if their value is preserved. Geopolitical 
tensions, the energy shock and the spread of artificial intelligence (AI) are putting 
both conditions to the test. Inflation is eroding the value of accumulated wealth, 
higher interest rates are changing its remuneration, and innovation is reshaping the 
opportunities and risks associated with its investment. 
1. The global economic outlook
The conflict in the Persian Gulf and its impact on the energy markets are 
affecting the global economy. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has driven fuel 
and gas prices to historically high levels, weighing on household budgets and 
driving up costs for businesses. Inflation has picked up again. The energy shock is 
now the main risk to the global economy.
Yet production has proved more resilient than expected, largely thanks to the 
boost that AI has provided to international trade, investment and equity prices. 
It is a revolution that is set to transform our economies and societies, and at the 
same time it is a competitive arena in which major powers vie for control of 
semiconductors and rare earths, and for access to energy.
However, this engine of growth is also a significant source of risk. Recent 
corrections in the equity prices of tech companies show just how rapidly 
expectations about the profitability of investment in AI can change.1 Scaling them 
back could have ripple effects across the global economy through trade, household 
wealth and investment.
Another risk to growth and financial markets is the pronounced increase in 
long-term government bond yields across the main economies (Figure 1). This 
reflects expectations of persistently high inflation and of tighter monetary 
conditions; delays in the necessary fiscal adjustment in several countries; and the

2. Monetary policy in the euro area
For central banks, these developments complicate the pursuit of price stability.
In the euro area, consumer price inflation has doubled since February, to 3.8 
per cent, almost entirely owing to the energy component. The pass-through of 
the shock to other goods and services has been limited so far: higher electricity 
production from renewable sources 4 has cushioned the impact of gas price hikes 
on firms’ costs. Core inflation barely exceeds pre-conflict values and the effects on 
expectations are limited to short horizons; wage growth has remained moderate.
Economic activity continues to expand. According to the European Central 
Bank’s (ECB) projections, GDP growth will be around 1.2 per cent this year and the 
next,5 while unemployment will fall to historical lows. The spread of AI is benefiting 
Europe too: in the first half of the year, the service sectors most closely linked to 
digitalization accounted for about one third of value-added growth in both the euro 
area and Italy; investment in intellectual property also increased in both areas.
The outlook for prices is worsening: overall inflation is expected to remain 
above 2 per cent for several quarters, while the increase in core inflation could 
become more pronounced early next year. 
The protraction of the crisis in the Middle East and pressures on the natural 
gas market, partly due to the need to rebuild European inventories, are driving risks 
to inflation upwards and risks to growth downwards. Against this background, the 
ECB Governing Council raised its key interest rates by 50 basis points between 
June and September, leaving them unchanged at yesterday’s meeting.

Monetary policy cannot prevent price increases caused directly by the energy 
shock. But it can and should prevent them from becoming entrenched in the expectations 
and behaviour of households and firms, triggering a wage-price spiral that would be 
costly to stop. Preserving price stability means safeguarding the real value of savings 
and protecting less well-off households, which are more exposed to persistent inflation.
With inflation set to remain above target for an extended period and risks skewed 
to the upside, further increases in the policy rates could prove necessary. The rise in  
long-term yields, mainly due to global factors, is making financial conditions tighter; 
this must also be considered when gradually calibrating monetary policy.
The Governing Council will continue to make decisions based on a  
data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting approach. This will make it possible to 
assess the transmission of the energy shock to core inflation, but also to adjust 
the stance promptly if the international situation were to improve or if financial 
tensions were to act as a brake on the economy and, thereby, on inflation. Such 
flexibility requires clear communication: explaining each decision transparently 
is essential to preserve trust in the ECB’s commitment to maintain price stability.
3. The European paradox: large savings and the technological gap
The European economy is currently squeezed between competition from 
China, now reaching into innovative sectors, and the lag vis-à-vis the United 
States in the AI supply chain.
The gap with the US economy has widened. Between 2019 and 2025, US value 
added grew at twice the pace recorded in Europe. As hours worked followed a similar 
trajectory, the difference can be explained by productivity (Figure 2), to which 
information and communication technologies (ICT) contributed significantly.6

Europe’s lag reflects lower investment in innovation. In the software sector, for 
example, over the past decade, the research and development expenditure of the most 
innovative US firms has gone from 8 to 17 times that of their European counterparts.7
Unless this trend is reversed, the gap could widen further. Such an outcome, 
however, is not inevitable. 
Europe continues to excel in the digital domain: while its lag in high-quality 
publications is smaller, it is more pronounced in patent production (Figure 3). The 
challenge is to harness these skills to develop high-tech goods and services and to 
nurture firms that can compete on the global stage. This requires greater public and 
private investment in the development and adoption of new technologies, as well as 
capital to finance innovative firms as they grow.
And yet, there is no shortage of resources. This is the European paradox: an 
economy that saves a great deal but struggles to channel those savings towards the 
innovation it needs.

Each year, the euro area saves one quarter of its gross national income, but 
a significant portion of these resources are invested abroad. Over the last decade, 
savings have exceeded domestic investment by an average of around €300 billion 
per year, equal to 15 per cent of productive investment.8 In the two years 2024-25, 
gross capital flows from the euro area to the United States averaged €785 billion 
annually, attracted in large part by the big tech firms.9
The problem, therefore, is not a shortage of savings, but the difficulty 
of deploying them profitably within Europe: what is lacking are specialized 
intermediaries, developed capital markets and adequate investment opportunities.
European households are less inclined to take risks than US households, but 
the gap is narrower than it appears looking at stock markets alone. In Europe, 
shares held through investment funds and listed shares held directly account for 5 
per cent of gross wealth, compared with 25 per cent in the United States. When

Much of the gap reflects the greater prevalence of unlisted family-owned 
firms in Europe. Investments in these firms, which are illiquid and concentrated, 
constrain portfolio diversification and reduce the scope for financing a broader 
range of innovative projects.
This is why, especially in the early stages of innovation, specialized 
intermediaries capable of pooling savings and diversifying risks – such as private 
equity and venture capital funds – are essential. Their development, which in the 
United States goes hand in hand with innovation, is being held back in Europe by 
a fragmented capital market. 
The time has come to deliver on the long-standing European commitment to 
building a truly integrated capital market.

The Savings and Investment Union Strategy goes in the right direction.   
It expands the role of institutional investors, facilitates their cross-border 
activities and strengthens market supervision at European level. 11 On this 
last aspect, the recent Ecofin agreement strikes a balance between greater 
integration and the role of national authorities. 12 When implementing it, it will 
be useful to model it on the Single Supervisory Mechanism, which combines 
strong European leadership with the expertise of national authorities: their 
knowledge of markets and intermediaries, on-the-ground experience and 
proximity to savers. 
Market integration also requires the completion of the banking union, 
including a common deposit insurance scheme that protects all depositors while 
taking into account the specific features of small banks. 13 Finally, it will also 
be necessary to create a safe and liquid European asset, not as a step towards 
fiscal union, but as a benchmark for investors that can attract foreign capital and 
strengthen the international role of the euro.
Concrete action can be taken at national level as well. The permanent 
forum recently announced by the Italian Ministry of Economy and Finance 
– which will bring together institutional investors, market participants and 
competent authorities to support the financing of innovation – is a step in 
this direction.

4. Savings, labour and pensions in Italy
Financial wealth needs to be put to better use in Italy too. Alongside this is 
another issue: household saving has declined over time, and those who need to 
save most, i.e. young people, have less chance of doing so. This is a key issue for 
the future well-being of an ageing society, in which supplementary pensions will 
increasingly have to augment public pensions.
The decline in savings recorded since 2000 is due to stagnating income and a 
lower propensity to set money aside (Figure 5).

The lower propensity to save is financially and demographically motivated. 
The decline in real interest rates following the launch of the euro has lowered the 
incentive to save; easier access to credit has also reduced the need to set aside

For those who are of working age today, however, greater life expectancy is 
another reason to put money aside: their savings need to finance longer periods of 
time and cover expenses that are hard to predict, especially for long-term care and 
welfare. The pension reforms of the last 30 years have also increased the role of 
private savings in supplementing income after retirement.
The decline in the propensity to save has come to a halt over the last 
decade, also because many households have begun to address these needs. 
Members of supplementary pension schemes have risen to over 10 million, 
equal to 40 per cent of the labour force; the financial resources accumulated 
account for over 11.5 per cent of GDP (Figure 6). 16

The difficulties are greater for young women, whose participation in the labour 
market is also hampered by the burden of family responsibilities.19
For the new generations, the fundamental answer is growth that can stabilize 
work paths and increase incomes: without continuity of contributions, no social 
security system can be fully effective.
Despite having made progress in recent years, supplementary pensions could 
also do more for young people. First of all, the number of members needs to be 
increased: automatic enrolment was reinforced by the latest budget law and is a 
step in the right direction.
In addition, participation in financial markets must be encouraged, adopting 
a long-term perspective. The recent proposals for saving tools that are accessible 
from birth are also noteworthy:20 starting to save early in life allows people to benefit 
for longer from the capitalization of yields and to adopt strategies in line with an 
extended horizon. The initiatives currently under discussion as part of the Budget 
Law appear to be consistent with this approach.21 To be successful, these tools should 
be simple and cheap, such as the savings and investment accounts (SIAs) proposed 
by the European Commission.

Conclusions
The European economy is showing more resilience than expected, as is the 
Italian economy. However, the outlook is clouded by the energy shock, as well as 
by geopolitical risks and financial tensions; the effects of inflation and of higher 
financing costs could escalate in the coming months.
Against this backdrop, the task of economic policy becomes more complex. 
Two basic needs remain unchanged: preserving price stability and maintaining a 
prudent fiscal policy, which are essential for protecting savings.
Yet they are not enough. Protecting savings requires an economy that can 
grow and generate income. This is the thread that links the issues discussed today: 
Europe and Italy have considerable wealth, but they are not able to fully exploit it 
to fund innovation and increase productivity. Without growth, it becomes difficult 
to support welfare, healthcare and social assistance in ageing societies; without 
growth, young people’s chances of finding suitable work, becoming independent 
and starting a family are limited.
The future of young people and the security of the elderly rest on the same 
ability to create well-being. It is up to all of us in our various roles to ensure that 
today’s savings support tomorrow’s growth and that young people, through work, 
can build themselves a future.
