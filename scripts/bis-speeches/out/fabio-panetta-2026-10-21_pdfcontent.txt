Introduction
Ladies and Gentlemen,
It is a pleasure and an honour to open the Green Swan Conference 2026, jointly organized 
by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, Banca d’Italia, 
and the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS). 
I would like to thank Governor Remolona and the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas for their 
generous hospitality. 
This year’s conference focuses on navigating climate risks amid uncertainty, and the topic 
could hardly be timelier. 
When the first Green Swan Conference was held in 2021, climate change was already 
recognized as a major long-term risk. Since then, the evidence has become more 
compelling. What was once regarded mainly as a threat to future generations is already 
affecting economic and financial conditions.
Mitigation remains indispensable, but it cannot eliminate physical climate risk, and 
some climate-related damage is already unavoidable. This makes resilience increasingly 
important.
In a world of greater climate uncertainty, we need to understand risks better, share and 
absorb them more effectively, and invest more decisively in reducing exposure and 
vulnerability. These are the three issues I will focus on today.

The economic case for strengthening resilience is clear. Climate change is reducing 
productivity, destroying physical capital and placing pressure on public finances. Extreme 
weather events caused more than USD 200 billion in direct damages in 2025, more than 
double the level recorded in the early 2000s.2 Climate-related shocks can also affect inflation. 
Their effects can be particularly severe in emerging market and developing economies, 
where exposure is often greater and the capacity to absorb shocks is more limited. 
Responsibility for climate policy rests first and foremost with governments. They have the 
democratic legitimacy and the instruments needed to guide the green transition, support 
adaptation and protect the most vulnerable. Climate policies must be economically 
viable, socially sustainable and institutionally credible. Overly rigid or frequently reversed 
policies can undermine public support and deter private investment.3
Central banks and supervisory authorities have a different but clearly defined responsibility. 
We need to understand how climate-related risks affect inflation, economic activity and 
financial stability, so that monetary policy, financial supervision and risk assessment 
remain effective.4 The challenge is not to expand our mandates. It is to fulfil our existing 
mandates in a changing environment.
1. Understanding climate risk
The first pillar of resilience is a better understanding of physical climate risk. Despite 
considerable progress, significant gaps remain in the availability, quality and comparability 
of climate-related data. Physical risks are highly localized and their impacts are often 
concentrated in specific periods, when extreme events trigger abrupt and disruptive 
damage. They also interact with economic and financial conditions in complex ways, 
while historical observations may become a less reliable guide as extreme events change 
in frequency and severity.
These features make climate risks difficult to identify, measure and price. Better information 
is therefore essential not only for assessing vulnerabilities, but also for directing capital 
and public resources towards the areas where adaptation is most needed.
Central banks and statistical authorities can contribute to this effort. The Irving Fisher 
Committee has long been working on climate-related data needs,5 and Banca d’Italia has 
been analysing data and methodologies to assess climate-related and environmental risks.6

Measurement is only the first step. Climate risks must also be integrated into financial 
institutions’ governance, internal controls and risk management. Many institutions have 
made progress, including in Italy7 and the euro area,8 but implementation remains uneven. 
Climate risk should not be treated as a separate compliance exercise. It should be 
integrated into the assessment of credit, market, operational and insurance risks.
Technology can strengthen this process. Artificial intelligence can improve climate 
modelling, weather forecasting, early warning systems and the assessment of extreme 
event risks.9 These tools cannot replace reliable data, sound models or human judgement. 
However, if used carefully, they can improve our ability to anticipate shocks and identify 
vulnerabilities before risks materialize.
We cannot eliminate climate uncertainty, but we can improve our capacity to understand 
it and prepare for its effects.
2. Sharing and absorbing climate risk
Understanding risk is essential, but risks that cannot be prevented must also be shared 
and absorbed effectively.
A large part of the losses caused by natural catastrophes remains uninsured. This 
protection gap leaves households and firms exposed to severe financial distress and 
often forces governments to intervene after disasters, at considerable fiscal cost.
The consequences can spread well beyond those directly affected. Households may 
be unable to rebuild; firms may interrupt production or default; public finances may 
deteriorate precisely when resources are most urgently needed. The shock can then 
propagate to banks and the wider economy.10
Insurance is therefore a central component of climate resilience. Its role goes beyond 
compensating losses. When risks are properly assessed and priced, insurance can also 
encourage prevention by providing financial incentives to reduce exposure to natural hazards. 
Expanding coverage is difficult. Households and firms may underestimate climate risks 
or consider insurance too expensive, and insurance markets remain underdeveloped in

Reducing the protection gap therefore requires a layered framework that combines 
private insurance, reinsurance, public backstops and capital-market instruments. Risks 
should be allocated to those best placed to bear them. 
Private insurers can cover smaller and more frequent losses. Reinsurers can absorb larger 
and more diversified exposures. Public authorities can provide backstops for extreme 
events that exceed private market capacity, while preserving incentives for prevention.
This approach was developed during Italy’s G7 Presidency in 2024 through the High-
Level Framework for Public-Private Insurance Programmes against Natural Hazards, and 
was reaffirmed under this year’s French G7 Presidency.11
Capital markets can further broaden risk sharing. Insurance-linked securities, including 
catastrophe and disaster relief bonds, can transfer risks beyond the traditional insurance 
sector, attract additional capital and diversify exposures.
To reduce reliance on ex-post government assistance, Italy has recently introduced 
compulsory insurance against floods, earthquakes and landslides for firms outside the 
agricultural sector, supported by a public backstop.12
The design of these arrangements is crucial. Coverage must remain affordable, premia 
should reflect risk and public intervention must preserve incentives for risk mitigation. 
The objective is to prevent losses from falling entirely on those least able to bear them or 
from threatening the wider financial system.
3. Reducing climate risk
Better measurement and more effective risk-sharing can limit the consequences of 
climate shocks, but resilience ultimately requires reducing exposure and vulnerability 
before disasters occur.
This means investing in adaptation.
The need is particularly urgent in countries that combine high exposure to physical risks 
with limited fiscal space. These conditions can create a dangerous feedback loop: higher

Breaking this vicious circle between climate and debt requires earlier and more effective 
action. Adaptation is generally less costly than repeated reconstruction, and should be 
seen as an investment that reduces future economic losses and fiscal pressures.
Yet adaptation remains severely underfunded, especially in emerging market and 
developing economies.15 There is growing recognition of the need to scale up adaptation 
finance, but translating international commitments into concrete projects and financial 
flows remains difficult. 16 A shortage of well-designed and investable projects, together 
with uncertain regulatory frameworks and weak institutional capacity can all deter private 
investment.
Mobilizing private capital therefore requires credible policies, clear incentives and an 
appropriate allocation of risks and returns between public and private actors.
Governments have a central role. They can invest in resilient infrastructure, improve 
planning and building standards, provide information on climate hazards and use public 
resources to attract private investment.
Multilateral development banks can support these efforts. Through guarantees, blended 
finance and other risk mitigation instruments, they can make adaptation projects more 
attractive to private investors and mobilize more capital than public financing alone.17 
Adaptation is shifting too slowly from reactive disaster response to proactive risk 
management. Progress remains uneven, while the frequency and severity of extreme 
events continue to increase.18

Technology can strengthen adaptation through advanced forecasting, early warning 
systems, remote sensing and data-driven infrastructure management. Yet technological 
progress is not without costs: the expansion of data centres is increasing the demand for 
energy, water and critical raw materials.19 These pressures must be managed so that digital 
innovation strengthens resilience without creating economic and social vulnerabilities.
Adaptation cannot prevent every disaster, but it can determine whether climate shocks 
remain manageable or not, and whether public resources are used to prevent losses or 
become repeatedly absorbed by reconstruction.
Conclusions
Ladies and Gentlemen,
Climate change is creating profound uncertainty, which makes preparedness increasingly 
important.
Resilience depends on our ability to understand risks, share them more effectively and 
reduce exposure before shocks occur. These elements reinforce one another: better 
information improves the pricing and allocation of risk, better risk-sharing strengthens 
the capacity to recover from adverse events, and adaptation reduces the losses that 
ultimately need to be absorbed.
No single institution can achieve these objectives alone. Governments, financial 
intermediaries, insurers, businesses, multilateral development banks and central banks 
have distinct but complementary responsibilities. 
International cooperation is equally essential. Climate shocks cross borders through 
trade, financial markets and supply chains, while the knowledge and solutions needed to 
strengthen resilience are often shared across countries.
The Green Swan Conference was created to foster this exchange among policymakers, 
academics and market participants. Today’s discussions can help us improve our 
understanding of climate risks, share effective solutions and accelerate investment in 
resilience.
Our economies and financial systems need to be able to absorb climate shocks without 
allowing them to develop into broader economic and financial crises. 
As the title of this address suggests, thinking about tomorrow means strengthening 
resilience today.
Thank you for your attention, and I wish you a productive and successful conference.
